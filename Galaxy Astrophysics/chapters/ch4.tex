\chapter{External Galaxies}

\section{Introduction}

The study of \textbf{external galaxies} marks a major advancement in astrophysics, expanding our perspective from the Milky Way to the Universe; however, this realization was reached gradually.

In the late 18th century, astronomers began cataloguing "nebulae"—a mix of galactic and extragalactic objects. The \textbf{Messier Catalogue} was one of the first, followed by \textbf{Herschel}'s list of roughly 5,000 nebulae in 1785. This effort continued into the late 19th century with \textbf{Dreyer}'s \textbf{NGC} (1888) and \textbf{IC} (1895), forming foundational resources for later work.

The landscape changed dramatically with new observational tools: larger telescopes and photographic plates. The 2.5-meter \textbf{Mount Wilson telescope} (1917) ushered in a new era of deep-sky, quantitative astronomy.

Concurrently, astronomers debated the structure and scale of the Milky Way. In 1922, \textbf{Kapteyn} developed a model in which the Galaxy was relatively small ($\sim8.5\,\mathrm{kpc}$ in diameter) and the Sun was near the center, largely because he didn't account for interstellar extinction (clarified by Trumpler in 1930). Meanwhile, \textbf{Shapley} (1918), analyzing the distances and distribution of globular clusters, concluded that the Milky Way was much larger, with the Sun far from the center.

This led to the historic \textbf{Great Debate} in 1920, where Shapley maintained all nebulae resided in a giant Milky Way, but Curtis argued that spiral nebulae were separate "island universes" (other galaxies). The issue was resolved by \textbf{Hubble}'s observations with the Mount Wilson telescope. In 1922, he identified Cepheid variable stars in the Andromeda "nebula" and, by measuring their periods and luminosities, estimated its distance at about $300\,\mathrm{kpc}$ (modern value: $\sim 600\,\mathrm{kpc}$). This placed Andromeda well outside the Milky Way and conclusively showed such nebulae are in fact external galaxies.

\section{Classification}

\subsection{Morphology}

The \textbf{morphological classification} of galaxies provides a systematic framework to describe their structure and appearance. The most widely used scheme is the \textbf{Hubble tuning fork diagram}, which organizes galaxies into distinct families based on their visual properties. Galaxies are broadly divided into: Ellipticals (E), Lenticulars (S0), Spirals (S), and Irregulars (Irr).

\begin{observationblock}[Projection effects]
    The observed morphology of a galaxy depends on its orientation with respect to the line of sight. A disk galaxy can appear circular (face-on) or highly flattened (edge-on), which must be considered when interpreting observations.
    \end{observationblock}

Let's see more in detail the main galaxy types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Elliptical galaxies (E)} \par
    Elliptical galaxies have smooth, featureless light distributions and are dominated by nearly elliptical isophotes, lacking prominent internal structure such as disks or spiral arms. The shape is quantified by the ellipticity,
    $$
    \epsilon = 1 - b/a
    $$
    where $a$ and $b$ are the semi-major and semi-minor axes, respectively. Ellipticals are classified as En, with $n = 10 \epsilon$ (for example, an E0 galaxy has $\epsilon \approx 0$, nearly circular; E4 has $b/a = 0.6$). They are found over a broad range of ellipticities ($0 \leq \epsilon \lesssim 0.7$). Ellipticals are typically populated by old stars, have little interstellar matter, and show little ongoing star formation.

    \item \textbf{S0 or lenticular galaxies} \par
    S0 galaxies are an intermediate or transitional type between ellipticals and spirals (sometimes called "lentil-shaped"). They include a central bulge similar to ellipticals and a large, faint, disk-like envelope, but lack the obvious spiral arm structure. S0s are subdivided into S0 and SB0, depending on the presence of a central bar. They contain old stellar populations; their disk component appears smooth, without prominent dust lanes or young stars.

    \item \textbf{Spiral galaxies (S, SB)} \par
    Spiral galaxies are composed of a flat, rotating disk containing spiral arms, a central bulge, and often a bar structure. They are subdivided into two main types:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Normal spirals} (S): Without a prominent central bar.
        \item \textbf{Barred spirals} (SB): With a well-defined bar in the bulge, from whose ends spirals emerge.
    \end{itemize}
    Both S and SB classes are further classified along a sequence (Sa, Sab, Sb, Sbc, Sc, Scd, Sd, etc). according to the relative prominence of the bulge and the openness of the spiral arms: early-type spirals (Sa, SBa) have large bulges and tightly wound arms, while late-type spirals (Sc, Sd, SBc, SBd) have smaller bulges and more open, patchy arms. The historical terminology "early" and "late" refers only to position along the sequence, not evolutionary state.

    \item \textbf{Irregular galaxies (Irr)} \par
    Irregular galaxies lack regular structure and do not fit into the elliptical or spiral schemes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Irr I}: Show weak or partial structure, possibly faint traces of spiral arms.
        \item \textbf{Irr II}: Have no discernible regularity and appear chaotic.
    \end{itemize}
    The spiral sequence may also be extended with transitional types such as Sdm, Sm (Magellanic spirals), and Im (Magellanic irregulars; e.g., the Large Magellanic Cloud is of type SBm). Irregulars typically show active star formation and considerable interstellar matter.

\end{itemize}

\begin{tipsblock}[Early vs.\ Late-type galaxies]
Elliptical and Lenticular galaxies are referred to as \textbf{early-type galaxies}, while spirals and irregulars are called \textbf{late-type galaxies}. This terminology was introduced by Hubble and is purely historical. In reality, galaxy evolution proceeds in the opposite direction along the Hubble fork diagram. Therefore, these terms \emph{do not} indicate an evolutionary sequence, and studies of galaxy evolution must account for changes in both kinematics and gas content.
\end{tipsblock}

\subsubsection{de Vaucouleurs Classification}

Beyond Hubble's classic scheme, a more nuanced and quantitative approach, the \textbf{de Vaucouleurs classification}, is often used in astronomical catalogues and research. This system builds on Hubble's ideas and introduces a numerical index, known as the \textbf{T-type}, to capture the continuous range of galaxy morphologies from ellipticals to late spirals and irregulars.
Each galaxy type is assigned a specific T value or range, enabling statistical or computational studies of galaxy populations:

\medskip

This classification is widely adopted in major galaxy catalogues, such as the \textit{Third Reference Catalogue of Bright Galaxies} (RC3). The numerical system reflects the observed continuum of morphologies and helps encode features such as bars or rings.

\subsection{Color Classification}

In addition to morphology, galaxies can also be classified according to their \textbf{color}, which reflects the properties of their stellar populations.

A widely used color index is $B-V$, representing the difference between blue ($B$) and visual ($V$) magnitudes. This index provides insight into the dominant stellar populations of galaxies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Red galaxies}: dominated by older, cooler stars. These are typically early-type galaxies (ellipticals and lenticulars), exhibiting little ongoing star formation and therefore redder colors.
    \item \textbf{Blue galaxies}: dominated by young, hot stars. These correspond mainly to late-type galaxies (spirals and irregulars), where active star formation is present, making them appear bluer.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
    \small
\textbf{Color} & \textbf{Types} & $\mathbf{B-V}$ \\
\hline
Red   & Elliptical, Lenticular & $\sim 1.0 - 1.5$ \\
Blue  & Spiral, Irregular      & $\sim 0.2 - 1.0$ \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

Dust plays a significant role in altering the observed colors of galaxies. It preferentially absorbs and scatters blue light more than red light, causing galaxies to appear redder than they actually are. As a result, while early-type galaxies (ellipticals and lenticulars) generally remain red due to their older stellar populations, late-type (blue) galaxies can also appear artificially redder if substantial dust is present along the line of sight.

\subsubsection{Galaxies color distribution}

A powerful tool for understanding galaxy populations is the \textbf{color-magnitude diagram} (CMD), which plots galaxy color (such as $B-V$ or $u-r$) against their luminosity or magnitude. Observationally, the CMD reveals a striking \textbf{bimodal} distribution: most galaxies cluster in either the \emph{red sequence}, composed of luminous, red galaxies (mainly ellipticals and lenticulars with older stellar populations), or the \emph{blue cloud}, where less luminous, bluer galaxies (mainly spirals and irregulars with ongoing star formation) reside.
Between these two prominent populations lies the so-called \textbf{green valley}, a relatively underpopulated region where galaxies are thought to be in a transitional phase, migrating from the blue cloud to the red sequence as they quench their star formation.

The observed color-magnitude distribution can be well described by the sum of two Gaussian functions, corresponding to the blue and red populations. The reddening (shift towards redder colors, mainly due to dust and evolving stellar populations) is particularly pronounced for the blue cloud galaxies, but also present in red sequence galaxies.

\subsection{SED Classification}

Galaxies can be broadly classified based on their energy and activity. Two major categories are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal galaxies}: These exhibit typical star formation rates (SFR), around $2\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$. Their emission primarily arises from stars spanning a range of ages and interstellar dust.
    \item \textbf{Active galaxies}: These include systems with either:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Starburst galaxies}: Undergo episodes of intense star formation, reaching SFRs as high as $100\,M_\odot\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}$, often triggered by mergers or interactions.
            \item \textbf{Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN)}: Host supermassive black holes at their centers that accrete matter, giving rise to highly energetic phenomena such as quasars, Seyfert galaxies, and radio galaxies, with strong emission across a broad spectral range.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

To further classify galaxies, astronomers analyze their \textbf{Spectral Energy Distribution (SED)}:

\begin{tipsblock}[Morphology vs.\ Color vs.\ SED]
    Morphology gives insight into a galaxy's overall structure and dynamics, whereas color provides a quick estimate of its dominant stellar populations and recent star formation. The SED synthesizes this information, capturing stellar, dust, and AGN components, and offering a more complete (though less directly visual) picture of a galaxy's properties.
\end{tipsblock}


\section{Surface Photometry of Galaxies}

Morphological classification describes \textit{what} galaxies look like; \textbf{surface photometry} quantifies \textit{how} their light is distributed on the sky. By measuring the surface brightness $\mu(R)$ as a function of projected radius $R$, one can fit structural models (bulge, disk, halo), compare galaxies of different types, and infer physical parameters such as scale lengths and concentrations.

\subsection{Brightness Profiles by Morphological Type}

The morphological families introduced above correspond to distinct, empirically measured brightness profiles. A convenient reference scale is the \textbf{effective radius} $R_e$, defined as the radius enclosing half the total luminosity ($L/2$), and the associated \textbf{effective surface brightness} $\mu_e \equiv \mu(R_e)$.

\subsubsection{Elliptical Galaxies}

Elliptical galaxies, from normal systems to giant ellipticals, are well described by a single structural component. Observationally, half of their luminosity is typically enclosed within an isophote of $\mu_e \approx 22\,\mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$, and the radial profile follows the \textbf{de Vaucouleurs profile} ($r^{1/4}$ law) already encountered for the Milky Way bulge. In logarithmic form, with $I_e \equiv I(R_e)$,
$$
\log\left(\frac{I(R)}{I_e}\right) = -3.3307\left[\left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/4} - 1\right]
$$
Expressing the same law in magnitudes, $\mu \equiv -2.5\log_{10} I$ (in $\mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$), gives
$$
\mu(R) = \mu_e + 8.3268\left[\left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/4} - 1\right]
$$

\begin{observationblock}[Two families of ellipticals]
In \textit{normal} ellipticals, the mean surface brightness within $R_e$ \textit{decreases} with increasing luminosity (brighter galaxies are more centrally concentrated). Dwarf ellipticals and dwarf spheroidals, by contrast, show the opposite trend: $\langle\mu\rangle_e$ \textit{increases} with luminosity.
\end{observationblock}

\subsubsection{Spiral Galaxies}

Spiral galaxies are composite systems. Half of the total luminosity typically falls within a region of somewhat higher surface brightness than in ellipticals, with $\mu_e \approx 21\,\mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$ in the $B$ band, reflecting the superposition of two structurally distinct components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bulge:} The bulge follows the same de Vaucouleurs law as elliptical galaxies:
    $$
    \mu_\mathrm{bulge}(R) = \mu_e + 8.3268\left[\left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/4} - 1\right]
    $$

    \item \textbf{Disk:} The disk is well approximated by an \textbf{exponential profile}, as for the Milky Way disk:
    $$
    \mu_\mathrm{disk}(R) = \mu_0 + 1.09\left(\frac{R}{h_R}\right)
    $$
    where $\mu_0$ is the \textbf{central surface brightness} (extrapolated to $R=0$) and $h_R$ is the \textbf{scale length}. A face-on spiral is therefore modeled as the superposition of a de Vaucouleurs bulge and an exponential disk; disentangling the two components is a central task in photometric decomposition.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The Surface Brightness of the Sky}

The first major complication in extragalactic surface photometry is that the sky itself is an extended source. Any measured galaxy profile must be compared against the \textbf{surface brightness of the sky} $\mu_\mathrm{sky}$: once the galaxy fades to roughly this level, its outer structure can no longer be traced reliably. Typical values under good observing conditions for $\mu_\mathrm{sky}$,  $[\mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}]$, (moonless) are:
$$
\sim 23\, (\mu_B) \quad \sim 22\, (\mu_V), \quad \sim 21\,(\mu_R)
$$
Since elliptical effective isophotes lie near $\mu_e \approx 22\,\mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$, the outer half of an E galaxy's light is already at the detection limit of ground-based imaging; deep, dark-sky observations are therefore essential for studying low-surface-brightness structure.

\subsubsection{Origin of the Sky Brightness}

The sky background is not a single physical source but the sum of several contributions along the line of sight:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Airglow}: photochemical emission in the upper atmosphere, variable with solar activity and local time.
    \item \textbf{Zodiacal light}: sunlight scattered by dust in the Solar System; typically the \textit{dominant} contribution at optical wavelengths.
    \item \textbf{Faint, unresolved stars}: individual stars below the resolution limit, producing a diffuse stellar background.
    \item \textbf{Extragalactic background light}: integrated emission from faint, unresolved galaxies; generally the smallest of the four components.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{warningblock}[Sky brightness as a hard limit]
Ground-based photometry cannot recover structure fainter than $\mu_\mathrm{sky}$. Features such as tidal tails, diffuse halos, and the outer disks of late-type spirals therefore require either exceptionally dark sites, space-based observatories, or long integrations that carefully subtract the time-variable sky background.
\end{warningblock}

\subsection{The Seeing Effect}

The second major complication is \textbf{atmospheric seeing}: turbulence in the Earth's atmosphere randomly deflects incoming light rays, blurring and distorting the image on timescales of milliseconds. The effect arises from rapid fluctuations of the refractive index along the optical path and is quantified by the \textbf{point spread function (PSF)}, which describes how a point source is smeared on the detector.

\begin{minipage}{0.54\textwidth}
    Mathematically, the observed surface brightness $I_\mathrm{app}(\vec{R})$ is the convolution of the intrinsic profile $I(\vec{R}')$ with the PSF $P(\vec{R}-\vec{R}')$:
    $$
    I_\mathrm{app}(\vec{R}) = \int P(\vec{R}-\vec{R}')\,I(\vec{R}')\,\dd^2{R}'.
    $$
    Deconvolving this relation to recover $I$ is ill-posed in general, because both the PSF and the true profile are only partially known; in practice one fits parametric models (de Vaucouleurs, exponential, Sérsic) directly to the convolved image.
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection{Modeling the PSF}

The PSF is often parameterized analytically. Two common choices are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{2D Gaussian} (a simple approximation for moderate seeing):
    $$
    P(d) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\,e^{-d^2/2\sigma^2}.
    $$

    \item \textbf{Moffat profile} (better suited to extended wings produced by atmospheric scattering). For a bivariate distribution centered at the origin,
    $$
    f(x,y;\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\beta - 1}{\pi\alpha^2}\left[1 + \frac{x^2+y^2}{\alpha^2}\right]^{-\beta},
    $$
    and for the corresponding radial distribution $r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}$,
    $$
    f(r;\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\beta - 1}{\pi\alpha^2}\left[1 + \left(\frac{r}{\alpha}\right)^2\right]^{-\beta}.
    $$
\end{enumerate}

\begin{minipage}{0.47\textwidth}
    Let $j(r)$ be the \textbf{3D luminosity density} at distance $r$ from the center of the galaxy, where $r$ is the true spatial radius.

    \medskip

    To connect the observed surface brightness $I(R)$ with the intrinsic $j(r)$, we sum up all emission along the line of sight at projected radius $R$. The line-of-sight coordinate is $z$, and for each $R$, $r^2 = R^2 + z^2$:
    $$
    I(R) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} \dd{z}\,j(z) = 2\int_0^\infty \dd{z}\,j(z)
    $$
\end{minipage}

\noindent Changing variables to $r$ via $z = \sqrt{r^2 - R^2}$, so that $\dd{z} = \dfrac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - R^2}}\,\dd{r}$, we get:
$$
I(R) = 2\int_R^\infty \frac{r\,j(r)}{\sqrt{r^2 - R^2}}\,\dd{r}
$$

\subsubsection{The Abel Integral}

Since the problem is spherically symmetric, this relation can be inverted using the \textbf{Abel integral} to recover the 3D luminosity density from the observed 2D surface brightness:
$$
j(r) = -\frac{1}{\pi r}\frac{\dd}{\dd r}\int_r^\infty \frac{I(R)\,R}{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}\,\dd{R}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
j(r) = -\frac{1}{\pi}\int_r^\infty \frac{\dd I}{\dd R}\frac{\dd{R}}{\sqrt{R^2-r^2}}
$$

\subsubsection{The Modified Hubble Law}

A widely used analytic form to describe the surface brightness profiles of galaxies is the \textbf{modified Hubble law}, which provides simple relations for both the projected (2D) surface brightness $I(R)$ and the intrinsic (3D) luminosity density $j(r)$. 

A key parameter in this law is the \textbf{scale radius} $r_0$, which sets the characteristic scale of the profile: $r_0$ is defined such that at $R=r_0$, the surface brightness drops to half its central value, i.e.\ $I(R=r_0) = I_0/2$. It therefore gives a measure of the concentration of light in the system.

The modified Hubble law can be written as:

\vspace{0.5em}

\begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{2D functional form (observed):}
    $$
    I(R) = \frac{I_0}{1 + \left(\dfrac{R}{r_0}\right)^2}
    $$
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
    \textbf{3D functional form (intrinsic):}
    $$
    j(r) = \frac{j_0}{\left[1+\left(\dfrac{r}{r_0}\right)^2\right]^{3/2}}
    $$
\end{minipage}

\noindent where $I_0 = I(R=0)$ is the \textbf{central surface brightness} and $j_0 = j(r=0)$ is the central luminosity density. In the outer regions, the profiles fall off as:
$$
R \gg r_0: \quad I(R) \propto R^{-2}, \qquad r \gg r_0: \quad j(r) \propto r^{-3}
$$

To compute the total luminosity of the galaxy out to a projected radius $R$:
$$
L(R) = 2\pi\int_0^R I(R')\,R'\,\dd{R'} = I_0 \pi r_0^2 \ln\left[1 + \left(\frac{R}{r_0}\right)^2\right]
$$

\section{Elliptical Galaxies}

\subsection{Classification}

Considering a population of elliptical galaxies, one can associate the observed volume density $\varphi$ with the theoretical luminosity function $\varphi_0$. Ellipticals are further subdivided based on their luminosity and structural properties:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal ellipticals}: comprising giant ellipticals (gE), intermediate-luminosity ellipticals (E), and compact ellipticals (cE). They span a wide range in absolute magnitude, from $M_B \sim -23$ to $M_B \sim -15$. About 50 galaxies are assigned to this class of early-type systems.

    \item \textbf{Dwarf ellipticals (dE)}: differ from cE's in that they have significantly smaller surface brightness and lower metallicity. They contain a substantial amount of dark matter (inferred from their mass-to-luminosity ratio, suggesting a large dark matter content within them).

    \item \textbf{Central Dominant galaxies (cD)}: extremely luminous (up to $M_B \sim -25$) and large (up to $R \lesssim 1\,\mathrm{Mpc}$) galaxies found only near the centers of dense clusters. Their surface brightness is very high close to the center, and they possess an extended diffuse envelope together with a very high mass-to-light ratio.

    \item \textbf{Blue Compact Dwarf galaxies (BCD)}: bluer than the other ellipticals, containing an appreciable amount of gas in comparison.

    \item \textbf{Dwarf spheroidals (dSph)}: very low luminosity and surface brightness, observed down to $M_B \sim -8$. So far they have only been observed in the Local Group.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Structure and Brightness Profile}

The de Vaucouleurs profile ($r^{1/4}$ law) describes giant ellipticals well:
$$
\log\left(\frac{I(R)}{I_e}\right) = -3.3307\left[\left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/4} - 1\right]
$$
with $L(<R_e) = \frac{1}{2}L_\mathrm{tot}$ and $I_e \equiv I(R_e)$. The total luminosity is:
$$
L = \int_0^\infty 2\pi R\,I(R)\,\dd{R} = 7.215\,\pi\,I_e R_e^2
$$
Combining the two relations above, one obtains an alternative form:
$$
I(R) = I_e\,e^{-7.669\left[(R/R_e)^{1/4} - 1\right]} \;\Longrightarrow\; I(R) = I_e\,f\!\left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)
$$

Giant ellipticals therefore tend to follow a \textbf{self-similarity law}: their structure can be fully rescaled by $I_e$ and $R_e$ alone.

\subsubsection{Elliptical Profile: The Kormendy Relation} \label{subsec:kormendy-rel}
 
For elliptical galaxies, the effective radius and the mean surface brightness within it are tightly correlated through the \textbf{Kormendy relation}:
$$
R_e \propto \langle I \rangle_e^{-0.83}
$$
This is one of several scaling relations (together with the Faber--Jackson relation and the fundamental plane) that constrain the structure and formation history of elliptical galaxies.

\subsubsection{Generalized de Vaucouleurs Profile (Sérsic Profile)}

To encompass the different light distributions, we can use the \textbf{Generalized de Vaucouleurs Profile}, also known as the \textbf{Sérsic Profile}:

$$
I(R) = I_e \, 10^{-b_m \left[ \left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/m} - 1 \right]}
$$

Here, the parameter $b_m$ is determined such that half of the total luminosity is within the effective radius: $b_m$ is set so that $L(<R_e) = L_{\text{tot}}/2$.

\begin{minipage}{0.52\textwidth}

The \textbf{Sérsic index} $m$ acts as a proxy for morphology:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$m = 4$}: Standard de Vaucouleurs profile, typical of luminous ellipticals (\textbf{E}; $M_V \approx -22.5$).
    \item \textbf{$m < 4$}: Dwarf ellipticals (\textbf{dE}) or spheroidals (\textbf{dSph}; $M_V \approx -17$). 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $m=1$: pure \textbf{exponential profile}
        \item $m=0.5$: \textbf{Gaussian profile} (rare)
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$m > 4$}: cD galaxies with extended envelopes.
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}%

\subsubsection{The Hubble-Oemler Law}

An alternative to describe the surface brightness profile is the \textbf{Hubble-Oemler Law}, which is a 3-parameter profile:

$$
I(R) = \frac{I_0 \, e^{-R^2/R_t^2}}{\left(1 + \frac{R}{r_0}\right)^2}
$$

Where $R_t$ acts as a truncation radius. This profile has specific behaviors depending on the radius:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the intermediate region where $r_0 < R < R_t$, the intensity drops roughly as $I(R) \propto R^{-2}$.
    \item The total integrated luminosity converges to a finite value, $L_\infty = L_{\text{tot}}$.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Hubble Law}

For the brightness profile, the \textbf{Hubble Law} provides a simple analytical form:
$$
I(R) = \frac{I_0}{\left(1 + \frac{R}{r_0}\right)^2}
$$
In the outer regions, where $R \gg r_0$, the intensity drops roughly as $I \propto R^{-2}$.

\subsubsection{The Hubble Modified Law}

To avoid the unphysical sharp peak at the center and introduce a constant brightness "core", the \textbf{Modified Hubble Law} enforces:
$$
\left. \frac{dI}{dR} \right|_{R=0} = 0 \quad \text{\{core\}}
$$

\subsection{Composition}

The composition of elliptical galaxies can be broken down into their stellar, gas, and dust contents:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Content of stars:} Ellipticals (E) and dwarf ellipticals (dE) generally appear red. This indicates an \textbf{old stellar population} dominated by K, M, MS, and giant red stars.
    \item \textbf{Color gradient and metallicity:} There is a color gradient present in both E and S0 galaxies. They are typically redder in the center because their \textbf{metallicity increases towards the center}.
    \item \textbf{Star formation:} There is evidence of a UV excess, pointing to a very small residual star formation rate ($\sim 0.5 \, M_\odot/\text{y}$).
    \item \textbf{Gas and Dust:} Only about $1\%$ of the total mass of an elliptical galaxy is made up of gas. However, there is a visible amount of gas, partially manifested as a \textbf{dust disk}. Both E and S0 galaxies are known to contain \textbf{warm and cold dust}.
    \item \textbf{Hot gas:} Ellipticals contain very hot gas ($T \sim 10^7 \text{ K}$), which is directly detected by its \textbf{X-ray emission}. This extended X-ray halo permits astronomers to measure the total mass of the galaxy.
    \item \textbf{Warm and cold gas:} In addition to the hot component, there are also \textbf{H$\alpha$ emission lines} from warm gas, as well as cold gas detected via \textbf{HI ($21\text{ cm}$)} and \textbf{CO molecular lines}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Kinematics of Elliptical Galaxies}

The internal dynamics of elliptical (E) galaxies are fundamentally distinct from those of spirals. Rather than being supported predominantly by systematic rotation ($V_{\text{rot}}$), ellipticals are maintained against gravitational collapse almost entirely by the \textbf{random motions} of their stars, characterized by their velocity dispersion $\sigma_v$. 

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
    Morphologically, the 3D shapes of elliptical galaxies are commonly divided into two idealized classes:

    \smallskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Prolate:} elongated along one axis.
        \item \textbf{Oblate:} flattened along the minor axis.
    \end{itemize}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.32\textwidth}
    \vspace{-0.5em}
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{assets/oblate-prolate.png}
\end{minipage}

    
    The degree of flattening of an elliptical galaxy is quantified by its \textbf{ellipticity}:
    $$
    \epsilon = 1 - \frac{b}{a}
    $$
    where $a$ and $b$ are, respectively, the semi-major and semi-minor axes. 
If the flattening were due solely to rotation, and if the orbits of the stars were isotropic, the ratio of observed rotation velocity to stellar velocity dispersion for an oblate, rotationally supported system is expected to follow:

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.51\textwidth}
    $$
    \left( \frac{V_{\text{rot}}}{\sigma_v} \right)_{\text{iso}} \sim \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}
    $$

\medskip


    However, observations show:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dwarf ellipticals (dE, $m \leq 4$, typically $m \sim 1$):} Exhibit substantial overall rotation: in some cases significant enough that rotation is the dominant support mechanism.
    \item \textbf{Luminous ellipticals (E, $m = 4$):} Show very little global rotation; in these galaxies, $V_{\text{rot}} \ll \sigma_v$.
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.46\textwidth}

    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/rot-mag.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}
    \captionof{figure}{Relative importance of rotation versus random motions in elliptical galaxies as a function of absolute magnitude $M_B$.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

In most luminous ellipticals, their flattened shapes stem mainly from \textbf{anisotropic stellar velocities}, not rotation. More massive ellipticals are typically more dispersion-supported and kinematically "hot," reflecting a history of mergers and violent relaxation.


\subsubsection{Complex Evolution}

Elliptical galaxies are not simply static, featureless ellipsoids; rather, they display a rich array of internal structures that shed light on their formation and evolution. Among these features are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Isophote twists:} Many ellipticals are triaxial, with the orientation of the isophotes' major axes varying with radius.
    \item \textbf{Counter-rotating disks:} Dwarf ellipticals (dE) can host gas disks that rotate opposite to the bulk stellar motion, and some show weak embedded stellar disks as well.
    \item \textbf{Boxy and disky isophotes:} The so-called \textit{boxiness parameter} quantifies how much an isophote deviates from a perfect ellipse. This property connects directly to the evolutionary history of the galaxy, particularly to mergers. Boxy ellipticals frequently arise from galactic mergers, which can be further classified into \textit{wet mergers} (involving both stars and gas) and \textit{dry mergers} (involving only stars).
    \item \textbf{Stellar kinematics and anisotropy:} The kinematics of stars depend on the sign of $a_4$; for example, boxy ellipticals can show counter-rotating stellar cores—a phenomenon rare in disky ellipticals. Also, the flattening of boxy ellipticals is primarily due to anisotropic stellar velocity distributions, which contributes to a broad range of X-ray luminosities ($L_X$).
\end{itemize}

An isophote tracing a perfect ellipse has coordinates $\theta_1 = a \cos(t)$, $\theta_2 = b \sin(t)$, where $t \in [0, 2\pi]$. The distance from a point on this ellipse to the center is:
$$
r(t) = \sqrt{\theta_1^2 + \theta_2^2} = \sqrt{\frac{a^2+b^2}{2} + \frac{a^2-b^2}{2}\cos(2t)}
$$

To capture the leading-order deviations from ellipticity, we can expand the shape in a Fourier series; the $\cos(4t)$ term represents the lowest-order correction that preserves bilateral symmetry. The perturbed isophote can thus be modeled as:
$$
\Theta(t) = \left( 1 + \frac{a_4 \cos(4t)}{r(t)} \right) \begin{pmatrix} a \cos(t) \\ b \sin(t) \end{pmatrix}
$$

The coefficient $a_4$ characterizes the isophote shape:

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$a_4 = 0$:} Pure ellipses, typically weak X-ray and radio emitters.
    \item \textbf{$a_4 > 0$:} Disky shapes, generally exhibiting mild rotation. About 70\% of ellipticals are disky; there may be a continuous transition from diskies to S0 galaxies as the disk-to-bulge ratio changes.
    \item \textbf{$a_4 < 0$:} Boxy shapes, which often show high mass-to-light ($M/L$) ratios and significant radio luminosity ($L_{\text{rad}}$).
\end{itemize}
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.35\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/boxiness.png}
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection{Mass of Ellipticals}

The total mass of elliptical galaxies is typically determined through two key approaches:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stellar Kinematics (Jeans Equations):} The motion of their stellar populations, governed by the Jeans equations (derived from the collisionless Boltzmann equation), provides insight into the underlying gravitational potential:
    $$
    n \frac{\partial \langle v_j \rangle}{\partial t} + \sum_i n \langle v_i \rangle \frac{\partial \langle v_j \rangle}{\partial x_i} = -n \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial x_j} - \sum_i \frac{\partial (n \sigma_{ij}^2)}{\partial x_i}
    $$
    \item \textbf{Hot Gas:} The X-ray emission from the hot interstellar gas allows direct measurements of the galaxy's total mass.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trends in the Sequence of Spirals}

As we move along the sequence of spiral galaxies (from early-type Sa to late-type Sc), several clear observational trends emerge:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{decreasing luminosity ratio} of the bulge compared to the disk:
    $$
    \frac{L_b}{L_d} \sim 0.3 \text{ (Sa)}, \quad \frac{L_b}{L_d} \sim 0.05 \text{ (Sc)}
    $$
    
    \item An \textbf{increasing opening angle} of the spiral arms, widening from $\sim 6^\circ$ in Sa to $\sim 18^\circ$ in Sc.
    
    \item An \textbf{increasing brightness structure} along the spiral arms:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Sa's:} Display a "smooth" distribution of stars along the spiral arms.
        \item \textbf{Sc's:} Display bright knots of stars and HII regions.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Compared to ellipticals, spiral galaxies cover a distinctly smaller range in absolute magnitude and mass:
$$
-16 \gtrsim M_B \gtrsim -23 \quad \text{and} \quad 10^9 M_\odot \lesssim M \lesssim 10^{12} M_\odot
$$

About $70\%$ of disk galaxies exhibit a large-scale \textbf{stellar bar}. Such bars break the axial symmetry of the gravitational potential, redistributing angular momentum among stars, gas, and dark matter, and can drive gas toward the center, potentially triggering nuclear activity.

\subsubsection{Brightness Profile}

The light profile of the bulge of spirals is described by a de Vaucouleurs profile to a good approximation, while the disk follows an exponential brightness profile, as is the case for our Milky Way. Expressing these distributions of the surface brightness in $\mu \propto -2.5\log(I)$, measured in $\mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$, we obtain:
$$
\mu_\mathrm{bulge}(R) = \mu_e + 8.3268\left[\left(\frac{R}{R_e}\right)^{1/4} - 1\right]
\qquad \qquad
\mu_\mathrm{disk}(R) = \mu_0 + 1.09\left(\frac{R}{h_R}\right)
$$

Here, $\mu_e$ is the surface brightness at the effective radius $R_e$, which encloses half the total luminosity. The disk's central surface brightness and scale length are $\mu_0$ and $h_R$, respectively.

Note that $\mu_0$ refers to the disk component only, and is not the true central surface brightness of the whole galaxy, nor is it directly observed.

\subsubsection{Bulge-Disk Decomposition}

Since the observed light of a spiral galaxy is the sum of a bulge and a disk component, the total surface brightness profile can be decomposed into these two contributions by fitting the de Vaucouleurs law in the inner regions and the exponential law in the outer regions. Plotted against $R$, the bulge component appears as the characteristic concave de Vaucouleurs curve, while the disk component appears as a straight line; the observed profile traces the brighter (i.e.\ numerically smaller $\mu$) of the two at each radius, smoothly transitioning from bulge-dominated to disk-dominated light.

\subsubsection{Bulges and Pseudobulges}

Not all bulges are alike. A distinction is made between classical \textbf{bulges} and \textbf{pseudobulges}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical bulges} are structurally and kinematically similar to elliptical galaxies: they are pressure-supported, dynamically hot systems whose light follows a de Vaucouleurs ($r^{1/4}$) profile.
    \item \textbf{Pseudobulges} are instead rotationally supported, disk-like structures, presumably built through secular evolution of the disk itself (e.g.\ via bar-driven gas inflow) rather than through mergers. Their light follows an exponential profile, much like the surrounding disk.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Pseudobulges are comparatively rare and more difficult to identify than classical bulges, since distinguishing the two requires a careful joint analysis of the surface brightness profile and the kinematics (rotation vs.\ velocity dispersion support) of the central component.

\subsubsection{Freeman's Law}

An intriguing empirical result, known as \textbf{Freeman's Law}, is that the central surface brightness of the disk component, $\mu_0$, is approximately constant across spiral galaxies of very different luminosities and sizes:
$$
\mu_0 = 21.52 \pm 0.39 \ \mathrm{mag/arcsec^2} \qquad (S_a \rightarrow S_c)
$$
$$
\mu_0 = 22.61 \pm 0.39 \ \mathrm{mag/arcsec^2} \qquad (S_d)
$$

In other words, the disks of essentially all spiral galaxies, regardless of the host's total luminosity, appear to share nearly the same central surface brightness. Since $\mu_0$ and $h_R$ are otherwise independent fit parameters, this constancy is a striking dynamical coincidence and not a mathematical necessity of the exponential profile itself.

\begin{tipsblock}[Selection biases and the use of Freeman's Law]
Freeman's Law is partly an effect of selection bias: galaxies with $\mu_0$ much fainter than $\sim 21$-$22\ \mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$ are simply harder to detect above the sky background, so historical samples were biased toward "Freeman disks". Modern deep surveys have revealed a population of genuine low surface brightness (LSB) disk galaxies that violate the law. Despite this caveat, Freeman's Law remains useful in practice: combined with the Tully--Fisher relation, it can help estimate the luminosity, and therefore the distance, of an observed spiral.
\end{tipsblock}

\subsubsection{Complex Tracers of Evolution}

\begin{center}
    \begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    In addition to their bulge and disk components, spiral galaxies display a rich variety of structural and kinematic features that reflect the processes involved in their formation and evolution.
        
        \medskip
    \textbf{Tidal interactions.} Close encounters and interactions with companion galaxies can perturb the stellar and gaseous disk, producing tidal tails, warps, and asymmetric spiral structure (e.g.\ \textbf{wraps}) that persist long after the encounter itself.

    \medskip

    \textbf{Stellar halo.} Both the Milky Way and M31 possess an extended, diffuse \textbf{stellar halo}, with a density profile that falls off as $\rho \propto r^{-3}$. If a spiral galaxy is sufficiently nearby and oriented edge-on, an additional \textbf{thick disk} component can also be detected, observed as a stellar population located beyond the scale height of the thin disk and above/below the plane of the disk.
    \end{minipage}%
    \hfill

    \centering

\end{minipage}%
\end{center}

\medskip

The thick disk of more distant spirals can only be studied if they happen to be oriented close to edge-on. For radii $R \lesssim 20\ \mathrm{kpc}$, the surface density profile of the stellar halo follows the extrapolation of the bulge's brightness profile; at larger radii, however, it exceeds this extrapolation, following instead a power-law profile $\rho \propto r^{-3}$. This indicates that stellar halos constitute a generic property of spiral galaxies, distinct from the bulge component itself.

\subsubsection{The Schmidt-Kennicutt Law}

The \textbf{Schmidt--Kennicutt Law} describes how the rate at which a galaxy forms stars depends on the amount of gas present in its disk. The surface density of star formation, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}$ (measured in $\frac{M_\odot}{\mathrm{yr}\;\mathrm{kpc}^2}$), increases as a power of the surface density of gas, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}$ (measured in $\frac{M_\odot}{\mathrm{pc}^2}$):

\large
$$
\Sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}} \propto \Sigma_{\mathrm{gas}}^{\,N}
$$
\normalsize
where $N \approx 1.4$.

\subsubsection{Surface Photometry Bias}

The observed surface brightness $\mu$ of a spiral galaxy can be strongly affected by the orientation and the distribution of dust in its disk, introducing important observational biases.

Consider first an idealized spiral galaxy with negligible dust. In this case, the total luminosity remains the same whether the galaxy is viewed edge-on or face-on. However, when seen edge-on, the emitted light is concentrated into a smaller apparent area on the sky (due to the thin vertical extent of the disk), so the surface brightness $\mu$ appears fainter compared to the face-on view.

Now, introduce significant dust into the spiral disk. For a dusty galaxy viewed edge-on, much of the starlight is absorbed or scattered out of the line of sight, reducing both the observed luminosity and the surface brightness. Intriguingly, the increase in extinction can offset the geometric effect: the surface brightness measured in edge-on and face-on configurations becomes nearly equal, despite the differing path lengths through the disk.

\subsubsection{The Disney Bias}

A selection effect occurs when comparing a galaxy's luminosity $L$ and its observed diameter $D$.

\begin{minipage}{0.47\textwidth}
    Disney originally noted an empirical relation:
    $$
    L \propto D^2,
    $$
    and argued that this relation could not be physical, but is instead an artifact of how galaxy samples are selected: surveys preferentially detect galaxies above some threshold surface brightness, since at fixed luminosity, a galaxy with a larger diameter $D$ has a correspondingly lower surface brightness $\mu \propto -2.5\log(L/D^2)$, eventually falling below the detectability threshold of the survey.
\end{minipage}%

As a result, low surface brightness galaxies of a given luminosity are systematically under-represented at the corresponding (large) values of $D$, biasing the observed relation toward $L \propto D^2$.

\subsubsection{Rotation Curves}

Like our own Milky Way, other spiral galaxies are observed to rotate considerably faster in their outer regions than one would expect from Kepler's law applied to the distribution of visible matter alone. Such measurements are typically obtained via the Doppler effect, where the inclination of the disk with respect to the line of sight must be properly accounted for in order to recover the true rotational velocity.

\medskip

From the distribution of visible matter alone, one would predict a circular velocity
$$
V_{\mathrm{lum}}^2(R) = \frac{G M_{\mathrm{lum}}(R)}{R},
$$
which should decline as $V_{\mathrm{lum}} \propto R^{-1/2}$ beyond the radius enclosing most of the luminous mass (the Keplerian regime). Instead, observed rotation curves remain approximately flat, $V_{\mathrm{rot}} \sim \mathrm{const}$, out to radii $R \gg h_R$, well beyond the visible disk, suggesting that spiral galaxies are surrounded by an extended halo of dark matter.

The discrepancy between the observed rotation curve $V^2$ and the luminous contribution $V_{\mathrm{lum}}^2$ yields the distribution of dark matter:
$$
V_{\mathrm{DM}}^2 = V^2 - V_{\mathrm{lum}}^2 = \frac{G M_{\mathrm{DM}}}{R}
\qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad
M_{\mathrm{DM}}(R) = \frac{R}{G}\left[V^2(R) - V_{\mathrm{lum}}^2(R)\right].
$$

The rotation curves of spiral galaxies cannot, therefore, be explained by visible matter alone; this is the classical starting point for the dark matter halo decomposition of a galaxy's mass budget.

\medskip

A common approach to interpret rotation curves is the so called \textbf{maximum disk hypothesis}. In this framework, the stellar disk is assumed to contribute maximally to the observed rotation curve, within the constraints imposed by the data. This corresponds to adopting a mass to light ratio $M/L$ consistent with stellar populations.

If the inferred $M/L$ is compatible with that expected from stars, the observed kinematics can be largely explained without invoking a significant dark matter contribution in the inner regions. On the contrary, if a much larger $M/L$ is required, this indicates the presence of an additional, non luminous mass component even within the disk.

Empirically, one finds that the maximum rotational velocity increases with the total luminosity of the galaxy. At fixed luminosity, however, variations in the rotation curve reflect differences in the total mass distribution, in particular in the relative contribution of dark matter.

The presence and structure of spiral arms are also closely related to the dynamics of the disk. Two main classes of spiral patterns are observed. 

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Grand design spirals} exhibit well defined, symmetric arms that can be interpreted as long lived density waves propagating within the disk. 

\item \textbf{Flocculent spirals}, on the other hand, show fragmented and irregular arm structures, which are often associated with local instabilities or tidal interactions with nearby galaxies.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Virial Theorem}

The virial theorem provides a fundamental relation between the kinetic and potential energy of a gravitationally bound system in dynamical equilibrium.

\begin{definitionblock}[Virial Theorem]

A \textbf{virialized system} is a gravitationally bound system that has reached \bfit{equilibrium}, so its overall properties (like the distribution of energies) are stable over time.

For such a system:
$$
2T + U = 0,
$$
where $T$ is the total kinetic energy and $U$ is the total gravitational potential energy.

\end{definitionblock}

\subsection{Proof}
Consider a system of $n$ point masses $m_i$, with position vectors $\vec r_i$ and velocities $\dot{\vec r}_i$. We define the virial quantity:
$$
A = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i \, \dot{\vec r}_i \cdot \vec r_i.
$$

Taking the time derivative:
$$
\dot A = \sum_{i=1}^n (\underbrace{m_i \dot r_i \cdot \dot r_i}_{2T} + \underbrace{m_i \ddot r_i}_{F_i} \cdot r_i)
= 2T + \sum_{i=1}^n \vec F_i \cdot \vec r_i
$$

Taking the time average over a sufficiently long interval $\tau$:
$$
\langle \dot A \rangle = \dfrac 1\tau \int_0^\tau \dot A dt =\langle 2T \rangle + \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^n \vec F_i \cdot \vec r_i \right\rangle.
$$

For a bound system, $A$ remains finite (no particle escapes), and therefore $\langle \dot A \rangle \to 0$ as $\tau \to \infty$. 

This yields the \textit{generalized form of the Virial theorem}:
$$
\langle 2T \rangle + \left\langle \sum_{i=1}^n \vec F_i \cdot \vec r_i \right\rangle = 0.
$$

For gravitational forces:
$$
F_i = -G m_i \sum_{j=1, j\neq i}^n m_j \frac{\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j}{r_{ij}^3} \qquad \text{where} \quad r_{ij} = |\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j|
$$

The latter term of the viral theorem becomes:
$$
\begin{array}{rl}
\sum_{i=1}^n F_i \cdot r_i 
& \displaystyle = -G \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j = 1, j \neq i}^n m_i m_j \dfrac {r_i - r_j}{r_{ij}^3} \cdot r_i\\
& \displaystyle = -G \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j = i + 1}^n m_i m_j \dfrac {r_i - r_j}{r_{ij}^3} (r_i - r_j)
\end{array}
$$

where the latter form is obtained by rearranging the double sum, combining the terms:

$$
\begin{array}{rl}
(1) & \displaystyle m_i m_j \frac{\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j}{r_{ij}^3} \cdot \vec{r}_i\\
(2) & \displaystyle m_i m_j \frac{\vec{r}_j - \vec{r}_i}{r_{ij}^3} \cdot \vec{r}_j = m_i m_j \frac{\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j}{r_{ij}^3} \cdot (-\vec{r}_j)\\
\end{array}
$$
since: $(\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j) \cdot (\vec{r}_i - \vec{r}_j) = r_{ij}^2$
$$
\Rightarrow -G \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=i+1}^n \frac{m_i m_j}{r_{ij}} = U
$$

where U is the potential energy of the system. Thus, the virial theorem becomes simply:
$$
\langle T \rangle = -\frac{1}{2} \langle U \rangle
$$

\subsubsection{Relation to Observables}

From the virial theorem, we can derive crucial \bfit{observational quantities}. By expressing the kinetic and potential energies in terms of individual particle masses $m_i$, velocities $v_i$, and spatial separations $r_{ij}$, the equilibrium condition becomes:
$$
\sum_i m_i (v_i - \langle v \rangle)^2 - G \sum_{i > j} \frac{m_i m_j}{r_{ij}} = 0
$$

To express this in terms of macroscopic observables, we can isolate the velocity dispersion and define a characteristic scale for the system. By factoring out the total mass $\sum m_i$, we obtain:
$$
\underbrace{\frac{\sum_i m_i (v_i - \langle v \rangle)^2}{\sum_i m_i}}_{\text{velocity dispersion}} - G \underbrace{\frac{\sum_{i > j} \frac{m_i m_j}{r_{ij}}}{(\sum_i m_i)(\sum_i m_i)}}_{\equiv \frac{1}{R_v}} \sum_i m_i = 0
$$

The first term represents the mass weighted velocity dispersion $\sigma_v^2$ of the system. The denominator of the second fraction defines the \bfit{virial radius} $R_v$. 

This definition allows us to rewrite the virial theorem as a direct relation between the total mass $M$, the three dimensional velocity dispersion, and the virial radius:
$$
\sigma_v^2 - G \frac{M}{R_v} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad M = \frac{\sigma_v^2 R_v}{G}
$$

Observationally, we cannot measure the full three dimensional velocity vectors or the true spatial separations of the components. Instead, we are restricted to projected quantities on the celestial sphere, specifically the two dimensional positions and the one dimensional radial velocities. Assuming the system is spherically symmetric and its velocity distribution is isotropic, the true velocity dispersion is exactly three times the observed line of sight variance:
$$
\sigma_v^2 = 3 \sigma_{v,\mathrm{los}}^2
$$

Similarly, the true spatial virial radius can be geometrically related to the projected virial radius $R_{v,\mathrm{proj}}$ by a constant factor:
$$
R_v = \frac{\pi}{2} R_{v,\mathrm{proj}}
$$

Substituting these projected quantities back into the mass equation yields a practical formula for estimating the dynamical mass of a spherical system:
$$
M = \frac{3\pi}{2} \frac{\sigma_{v,\mathrm{los}}^2 \, R_{v,\mathrm{proj}}}{G}
$$

A significant practical challenge arises because the individual masses $m_i$ of the tracers are generally unknown. Astronomers often substitute mass weighting with luminosity weighting under the assumption that mass is strictly proportional to luminosity. However, this assumption is not always valid due to observational selection effects and the segregation of masses within the system. When luminosity weighting is unreliable, an unweighted approach is preferred, calculating simple averages of the observed velocities and projected radii.

\begin{observationblock}[Mass Distributions and Advanced Theorems]
The standard virial mass estimator implicitly assumes that the underlying mass distribution perfectly follows the spatial distribution of the observed luminous tracers. In systems like galaxy clusters, where the dominant dark matter component may be distributed differently than the visible galaxies, this assumption breaks down. To address these complex mass distributions and handle systems lacking spherical symmetry, astronomers must rely on more advanced frameworks such as the \bfit{tensorial virial theorem} or the \bfit{generalized virial theorem}.
\end{observationblock}

Finally, when analyzing discrete systems, it is often useful to define the \bfit{harmonic radius} $R_H$. Assuming equal mass particles for simplicity, the definitions of the virial radius and the harmonic radius are:
$$
R_v = \frac{n^2}{\sum_{i>j} \frac{1}{r_{ij}}} \qquad \text{and} \qquad R_H = \frac{\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)}{\sum_{i>j} \frac{1}{r_{ij}}}
$$

For a system with a large number of particles $n$, the virial radius is approximately twice the harmonic radius, meaning $R_v \approx 2 R_H$. Applying this substitution provides an alternative expression for the total dynamical mass using the harmonic radius:
$$
M = \frac{3\pi}{2} \frac{\sigma_{v,\mathrm{los}}^2 R_v}{G} = 3\pi \frac{\sigma_{v,\mathrm{los}}^2 R_H}{G}
$$

\section{Scaling Relations}

Scaling relations are empirical correlations between the global observable properties of galaxies. They are essential tools in astrophysics because they provide deep insights into galaxy formation and serve as crucial secondary distance indicators.

\subsection{The Tully Fisher Relation}

The \textbf{Tully Fisher relation} provides a powerful tool to relate the intrinsic luminosity of a spiral galaxy to its kinematic properties, specifically its maximum rotational velocity $V_{max}$:
$$
L \propto V_{max}^\alpha \qquad \text{where} \quad \alpha \approx 4
$$

This relation is calibrated with nearby galaxies whose distances are measured using primary indicators like Cepheids. For intermediate distance galaxies where stars aren't individually resolved, the Tully Fisher relation bridges the gap. By observing a galaxy's spectrum, we measure its redshift and rotation curve (to get $V_{max}$). The relation then provides the absolute luminosity $L$ (or magnitude $\mathcal{M}$), which, with the apparent magnitude $m$, yields the distance.

The physical basis of the Tully Fisher relation comes from combining the virial theorem with the properties of spiral disks. In virial equilibrium, the dynamical mass $M$ is proportional to the rotational velocity squared times the characteristic radius $R$:
$$
M \propto \frac{V_{max}^2 R}{G}
$$

We can introduce the luminosity $L$ by multiplying and dividing by the mass to light ratio:
$$
L = M \left( \frac{M}{L} \right)^{-1} \propto \frac{V_{max}^2 R}{G} \left( \frac{M}{L} \right)^{-1}
$$

Squaring both sides gives:
$$
L^2 \propto \left( \frac{M}{L} \right)^{-2} \frac{R^2}{G^2} V_{max}^4
$$

For a disk galaxy, the total luminosity is related to the average surface brightness $\langle I \rangle$ and the square of the radius, meaning $R^2 \propto L / \langle I \rangle$. Substituting this into our squared expression yields:
$$
L^2 \propto \left( \frac{M}{L} \right)^{-2} \frac{L}{\langle I \rangle G^2} V_{max}^4
$$

Dividing both sides by $L$ leaves the final theoretical expectation:
$$
L \propto \left( \frac{M}{L} \right)^{-2} \left(\frac{1}{\langle I \rangle G^2} \right) V_{max}^4
$$

If we assume the mass-to-light ratio $M/L$ and the mean surface brightness $\langle I \rangle$ do not vary significantly between galaxies (as approximated by the Freeman law, $M/L \sim \mathrm{const}$), then the relation simplifies to:
$$
L \propto V_{max}^4
$$

It is important to note, however, that in real observations the exponent $\alpha$ is not exactly 4, and its precise value depends somewhat on the photometric band used (e.g., B-band, K-band).

\subsection{The Faber Jackson Relation} \label{subsec:fj-rel}

While spiral galaxies follow the Tully Fisher relation, elliptical galaxies exhibit a similar kinematic scaling known as the \textbf{Faber Jackson relation}. For ellipticals, the relevant kinematic parameter is the central velocity dispersion $\sigma_0$, which represents the random thermalized motion of the stars.

The empirical \textbf{Faber-Jackson relation} connects the total luminosity in the visual band $L_V$ to the central velocity dispersion $\sigma_0$:
$$
L_V \propto \sigma_0^\gamma \qquad \text{where} \quad \gamma \approx 4
$$

A commonly used empirical form relates $\sigma_0$ to the absolute magnitude $M_B$ in the $B$-band:
$$
\log(\sigma_0) = -0.1 M_B + \text{const}
$$

\subsection{The Fundamental Plane}

The \href{subsec:fj-rel}{Faber Jackson Relation} and the \href{subsec:kormendy-rel}{Kormendy Relation} are actually just two dimensional projections of a three dimensional parameter space, which is defined by three quantities: the effective radius $R_e$, the central velocity dispersion $\sigma_0$, and the average surface brightness within the effective radius $\langle I \rangle_e$. When plotted in a three dimensional logarithmic space, elliptical galaxies do not scatter randomly but populate a tightly constrained surface known as the \textbf{Fundamental Plane}.

Observationally, the fundamental plane is described by the empirical relation:
$$
R_e \propto \sigma_0^{1.4} \langle I \rangle_e^{-0.85}
$$

We can understand the origin of this plane returning to the virial theorem and kormendy relation, which dictate respectively:
$$
M \propto \frac{\sigma_0^2 R_e}{G}, \qquad \qquad R_e \propto \langle I \rangle_e^{-0.83}
$$

Combining this with the definition of total luminosity, where $L \propto \langle I \rangle_e R_e^2$, we can express the effective radius in terms of the other variables and the mass to light ratio:
$$
R_e \propto \sigma_0^2 \, \langle I \rangle_e^{-1} \left( \frac{M}{L} \right)^{-1}
$$

By comparing this theoretical virial expectation with the empirical fundamental plane, we notice a discrepancy known as the \textit{tilt of the fundamental plane}. The theoretical exponents are $2$ and $-1$, whereas the observed ones are $1.4$ and $-0.85$. This implies that the mass to light ratio is not strictly constant for all ellipticals, but rather scales weakly with the total mass, approximately following:
$$
\frac{M}{L} \propto M^{0.2}
$$


\subsection{The $\mathbf{D_n}$-$\sigma$ Relation}

To bypass the complexities of precise surface brightness measurements required by the fundamental plane, astronomers introduced the \textbf{$\mathbf{D_n}$-$\boldsymbol{\sigma}$ relation} as an optimized and robust distance indicator for early type galaxies. Here, the parameter $D_n$ is defined as the physical diameter of the galaxy within which the average integrated surface brightness corresponds to exactly $20.75 \, \mathrm{mag/arcsec^2}$.

This new parameter correlates tightly with the central velocity dispersion according to the relation:
$$
D_n \propto \sigma_0^{1.33}
$$

This single relation elegantly combines the structural variables of the fundamental plane. First, one measures the central velocity dispersion $\sigma_0$ from the galactic spectrum, which immediately yields $D_n$. Next, photometric imaging is used to find the apparent angular diameter $\theta_n$ on the sky where the surface brightness threshold is met. Finally, the physical distance to the galaxy is simply determined from the ratio of the physical diameter to the observed angular diameter.

\section{Luminosity Function of Galaxies}

The luminosity function $\Phi(L)$ (or $\Phi(M)$), specifies the statistical distribution of a given class of celestial objects with respect to their intrinsic luminosity or absolute magnitude. More precisely, the luminosity function defines the number density of galaxies at a specific luminosity.

The total number density of galaxies in a given volume can be obtained by integrating the luminosity function over all possible luminosities or magnitudes:
$$
\nu = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Phi(M) \, dM = \int_0^\infty \Phi(L) \, dL
$$

Deriving an accurate luminosity function observationally involves several significant challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distance Estimates:} essential to convert apparent magnitudes into absolute luminosities. This is easier for galaxies within a cluster, as they all share approximately the same distance. For isolated galaxies, however, it requires measuring redshifts and assuming a cosmological model.
    \item \textbf{Large Scale Structure:} The Universe is not perfectly homogeneous on small scales. To obtain a statistically representative sample of the galaxy population, the survey must encompass a sufficiently large volume to average out local cosmic voids and superclusters.
    \item \textbf{Malmquist Bias:} In any flux limited survey, highly luminous galaxies will always be systematically overrepresented. Because they are intrinsically brighter, they can be observed out to much larger distances and thus sampled over a much larger volume than faint dwarf galaxies.
\end{itemize} 

\subsection{The Schechter Luminosity Function (General Luminosity Function)}

Over the decades, astronomers have developed analytical formulas to fit the observed distributions of mass and light. In 1974, Press and Schechter developed a theoretical mass function for dark matter halos. Later, in 1976, Schechter proposed a highly successful empirical formula for the luminosity function of galaxies. 

The \textbf{Schechter Luminosity Function} describes a power law distribution at faint luminosities combined with a rapid exponential cutoff at high luminosities:
$$
\Phi(L) \, dL = \left( \frac{\Phi^*}{L^*}\right) \left( \frac{L}{L^*} \right)^{\alpha} e^{-\left( \frac{L}{L^*} \right)} \frac{dL}{L^*}
$$
This function relies on three fundamental parameters:

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\Phi^*$:} The normalization constant. It dictates the overall number density of galaxies.
    \item \textbf{$L^*$ (or $M^*$):} The characteristic luminosity. It represents the "knee" of the function, separating the low luminosity power law behavior from the high luminosity exponential drop off.
    \item \textbf{$\alpha$:} The faint end slope parameter. It is typically negative ($\alpha \sim -1$), indicating that the number density continues to rise as luminosity decreases.
\end{itemize}
    \end{minipage}
    \hfill
    \begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/schechter.png}
    \end{minipage}

\vspace{-0.5em}

\begin{exampleblock}[Empirical Fits in K and B Bands]
The parameters of the Schechter function vary depending on the observed wavelength band.

\begin{itemize}
\item For the optical \textbf{B band} (where $h$ is the dimensionless Hubble parameter):

\vspace{-0.5em}

$$
\Phi^*_B = 1.6 \times 10^{-2} \, h^3 \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}
$$

\vspace{-0.8em}

$$
M^*_B = -19.7 + 5 \log_{10} h \quad \Rightarrow \quad L^*_B = 1.2 \times 10^{10} \, h^{-2} \, L_{\odot, B}
$$
The faint end slope in the B band is typically found to be $\alpha_B \approx -1.07$.

\item For the near infrared \textbf{K band}:

\vspace{-0.5em}

$$
\Phi^*_K = 1.6 \times 10^{-2} \, h^3 \, \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}
$$

\vspace{-0.8em}

$$
M^*_K = -23.1 + 5 \log_{10} h
$$
The faint end slope here is slightly shallower, around $\alpha_K \approx -0.9$.
\end{itemize}
\end{exampleblock}

Using the Schechter function, we can calculate total integrated quantities for a given volume. The total luminosity density (the sum of all light emitted by all galaxies) is given by:
$$
L_{\mathrm{tot}} = \int_0^\infty L \, \Phi(L) \, dL = \Phi^* L^* \, \Gamma(2 + \alpha), \qquad \text{where } \Gamma = \int_0^\infty dy y^{x-1}e^{-y}
$$
For the total luminosity to be finite and physically meaningful, the integral requires $\alpha > -2$. Interestingly, the majority of the light in the Universe is produced by galaxies with luminosities near $L^*$. Specifically, $60\%$ of the total luminosity is emitted by galaxies in the range $0.22 L^* < L < 1.6 L^*$, and $90\%$ is contained within the bounds $0.1 L^* < L < 2.3 L^*$.

Similarly, we can calculate the total number density of galaxies:
$$
N_{\mathrm{tot}} = \int_0^\infty \Phi(L) \, dL = \Phi^* \, \Gamma(1 + \alpha)
$$
This integral diverges (becomes infinite) if $\alpha \leq -1$. Since observational fits often yield $\alpha \approx -1$, the implication is that the Universe contains an enormous number of extremely faint dwarf galaxies.

\medskip

The typical number density normalization for field galaxies is $\Phi^* \sim 2 \times 10^{-2} \mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}$, which corresponds to an average separation between galaxies of $\sim4 \mathrm{Mpc}$. However, in dense region the density is much higher than in the isolated field, reducing theis distance to roughly $1$ to $2 \mathrm{Mpc}$.
