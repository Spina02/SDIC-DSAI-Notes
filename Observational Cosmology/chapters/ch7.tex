\chapter{Cosmological Probes} \label{ch:cosmo_probes}

\section{The $\Lambda$CDM Framework} \label{sec:lcdm}

\subsection{Assumptions and Observational Pillars} \label{subsec:lcdm_assumptions}

Modern cosmology is founded on the \bfit{$\Lambda$CDM model}, a framework that explains the Universe's evolution from the Big Bang to today using just a few free parameters. Its key assumptions are: gravity follows General Relativity (GR); particle physics is described by quantum field theory (QFT); the Universe obeys the \textbf{cosmological principle} (isotropy and homogeneity); the early Universe had a rapid expansion phase (\textbf{inflation}); most matter is cold and collisionless (\textbf{dark matter}); and today's accelerated expansion is driven by a component with negative pressure, labeled $\Lambda$. Each of these assumptions is backed by observations. General Relativity is supported by Solar System tests (e.g., Mercury's perihelion, light deflection, gravitational redshift), timing of binary pulsars (Hulse-Taylor), and, recently, by direct detection of gravitational waves from compact-object mergers, confirming GR even in the strong-field regime.

\subsubsection{The Cosmological Principle} \label{subsubsec:cosmo_principle}

The \textbf{cosmological principle} states that the Universe is \textbf{isotropic and homogeneous} on sufficiently large scales, of order $100\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}$. Isotropy, the absence of a preferred direction, is directly established by observation: the CMB temperature field is uniform to a few parts in $10^5$ once the dipole is removed, and galaxy surveys reveal no preferred axis on sufficiently large volumes. Homogeneity cannot be verified directly from a single vantage point and is instead adopted as an extension of the \textbf{Copernican principle}: since there is no reason to suppose that our location in the Universe is special, an isotropic Universe as seen from every point must also be homogeneous.

\subsubsection{Cosmic Inflation} \label{subsubsec:inflation}

\textbf{Inflation} is a brief phase of exponential expansion (about 60 e-folds) at $t \sim 10^{-37}$-$10^{-35}\,$s after the Big Bang, proposed to solve three key puzzles of standard cosmology.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
The \textbf{horizon problem} concerns the near-perfect uniformity of the CMB temperature across the sky. In a Universe without inflation, causally disconnected regions at recombination subtend an angular scale of only $R_H(z_{\text{rec}}) \sim 1^\circ$ on the sky today, since light could not have travelled far enough since the Big Bang to connect them; yet regions separated by tens of degrees are observed to share the same temperature to within $10^{-5}$. Inflation resolves this by pushing the effective onset of the causal, particle horizon far earlier in conformal time, so that regions now widely separated on the sky were in causal contact before inflation began.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.47\textwidth}
    % \vspace{-0.7em}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{The horizon problem: regions separated at recombination are linked by a prior inflationary epoch.}
    \label{fig:horizon_problem}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
The \textbf{flatness problem} asks why the Universe appears so close to spatially flat today ($\Omega_k \simeq 0$). In a decelerating Universe, flatness ($\Omega_{\text{tot}} = 1$) is \emph{unstable}: any tiny deviation from flatness in the early Universe would quickly grow, so observing near-exact flatness now would require extreme initial fine-tuning.
Inflation solves this: rapid exponential expansion stretches space so much that any curvature is flattened away, driving $\Omega_{\text{tot}} \to 1$ no matter its starting value, like zooming in on a balloon's surface, which looks flatter as its radius increases.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.43\textwidth}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \label{fig:flatness_problem_minipage}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

Finally, the \textbf{magnetic monopole problem}: many grand unified theories predict large numbers of stable, superheavy magnetic monopoles in the early Universe, yet none have been observed. Inflation solves this by exponentially diluting any relics present before it begins, reducing their number density by $e^{3N}$ (with $N \sim 60$), so that any primordial monopoles are undetectable today.

\subsubsection{Dark Matter} \label{subsubsec:dark_matter_evidence}

\bfit{Dark matter} is a hypothesized massive particle that interacts with ordinary matter and radiation primarily through gravity. Although it has never been directly detected, strong evidence for its existence comes from many independent observations on different scales.

\medskip

The first hint came from Zwicky's 1933 study of the Coma cluster: the observed galaxy velocities implied much more total mass than could be explained by the visible galaxies. Later, Vera Rubin measured \textbf{galaxy rotation curves} (\cref{sec:ism_hi}). Instead of declining at large radii as expected, galaxy rotation speeds remain nearly flat, implying an extended, invisible ("dark") mass component.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
Further evidence comes from gravitational lensing and the growth of structure. In merging clusters like the Bullet Cluster, gravitational lensing shows the total mass is offset from the X-ray-emitting gas (which marks most of the baryonic mass), directly indicating that most of the mass is dark and collisionless. Additionally, structure formation (going from tiny CMB fluctuations to the dense cosmic web today) can only be explained if most collisionless matter decouples early from the photon-baryon fluid; if the Universe were made of only baryons, there would not have been enough time for such structures to grow.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.40\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/CDM-growth.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Growth of CDM, baryon, and photon density perturbations.}
    \label{fig:dm_lensing_growth}
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection{Accelerated Expansion and Dark Energy} \label{subsec:dark_energy_discovery}

The final critical pillar of the $\Lambda$CDM model is the discovery that the Universe's expansion is not just continuing, but actually accelerating—meaning galaxies are receding from each other faster and faster over time. This remarkable fact was firmly established in 1998-1999 by two independent teams, the High-Z Supernova Search Team and the Supernova Cosmology Project (2011 Nobel Prize). They found that distant Type Ia supernovae (\cref{sec:standard_candles}) appeared systematically fainter than they would in a Universe containing only matter. The simplest explanation for this dimming is that the expansion of the Universe has sped up over time, requiring the existence of an unknown component with strong negative pressure. This mysterious phenomenon is known as \textbf{dark energy}, and in the simplest scenario it is described mathematically by a cosmological constant $\Lambda$.

\subsection{Current Status and Open Tensions} \label{subsec:lcdm_status}

Today, multiple precise and independent cosmological probes—ranging from the CMB angular power spectrum at $z \simeq 1100$ to the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) pattern in large galaxy surveys at low redshift—consistently support a spatially flat $\Lambda$CDM Universe. Under the flatness condition,
$$
\Omega_k = 1 - \sum_i \Omega_i = 0
$$
the standard $\Lambda$CDM cosmology is fully characterised by six fundamental parameters (with a seventh included for massive neutrinos):
$$
\boxed{
    \Lambda\mathrm{CDM}+\nu \text{parameters:}
    \textcolor{orange}{\Omega_m, \Omega_b,~\Omega_\nu},
    \textcolor{OliveGreen}{H_0},
    \textcolor{MidnightBlue}{\sigma_8},
    \textcolor{violet}{\tau},
    \textcolor{ForestGreen}{n_s}
}
$$

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textcolor{orange}{$\Omega_m$, $\Omega_b$, $\Omega_\nu$}: energy density parameters (total, baryons, neutrinos) relative to the critical density;
    \item \textcolor{OliveGreen}{$H_0$}: the Hubble constant, setting the current expansion rate of the Universe;
    \item \textcolor{MidnightBlue}{$\sigma_8$}: the amplitude of matter fluctuations on $8\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}$ scales;
    \item \textcolor{violet}{$\tau$}: the optical depth to reionisation;
    \item \textcolor{ForestGreen}{$n_s$}: the spectral index of the primordial fluctuation spectrum.
\end{itemize}

The present-day energy budget of the Universe, as measured by these probes, is dominated by dark energy ($\sim 69\%$) and cold dark matter (CDM, $\sim 26\%$), with only about $5\%$ in ordinary (baryonic) matter and roughly $1\%$ in neutrinos.

\medskip

Despite this concordance, the two main components (dark matter and dark energy) remain deeply mysterious. We do not know the particle nature of dark matter, nor whether cosmic acceleration is driven by a true dark energy component, a modification of gravity, or something else. Fundamentally, it is also unknown if dark energy is a true constant (as in $\Lambda$CDM) or evolves with time (as in $w$CDM). If dark energy is vacuum energy, its observed value is inexplicably tiny compared to predictions from quantum field theory: $\frac{\rho_{\Lambda,\,\text{QFT}}}{\rho_{\Lambda,\,\text{observed}}} \approx 10^{120}$. The physical origin of inflation is also unknown.

\smallskip

Compounding these open theoretical questions, precision measurements over the past decade have revealed statistically significant tensions between parameters inferred from early-Universe probes (the CMB) and those inferred from late-time, low-redshift probes (supernovae, cosmic shear, galaxy clustering, and cluster counts). The most discussed of these is the \bfit{Hubble tension}: local, late-Universe determinations of $H_0$ from the distance ladder (e.g. $H_0 \simeq 74 \pm\,\text{km\,s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1}$) sit some $4$-$6\sigma$ above the value inferred from the Planck CMB under $\Lambda$CDM ($H_0 \simeq 67\,\text{km\,s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1}$). A related, if somewhat milder, tension exists in the amplitude of matter clustering, commonly parametrised as $S_8 \equiv \sigma_8\sqrt{\Omega_m/0.3}$: weak-lensing and cluster-based measurements of $S_8$ tend to fall systematically below the value predicted by the Planck CMB best-fit model.

\smallskip

\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/CDM-tension.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{The Hubble tension (left) and the $S_8$ tension (right): discrepancies between early- and late-Universe determinations of key $\Lambda$CDM parameters.}
    \label{fig:h0_s8_tension}
\end{center}

Most recently, results from the DESI spectroscopic survey, combining BAO measurements with the CMB and with independent SN Ia compilations, have provided tentative evidence that the equation of state of dark energy may not be exactly constant, hinting at a preference for a time-evolving dark energy component (parametrised as $w_0 w_a\text{CDM}$) over the simplest cosmological-constant scenario. Whether this preference reflects genuinely new physics, an unaccounted-for systematic, or a statistical fluctuation remains an open and actively debated question.

\begin{tipsblock}[A strategy for stress-testing $\Lambda$CDM]
A robust test of the $\Lambda$CDM model combines early-Universe probes (such as the CMB) with diverse late-time measurements across a wide redshift range. Expansion history and structure growth respond differently to cosmological parameters, particularly dark energy. By comparing multiple independent observables spanning broad redshifts, we can reveal discrepancies between models that may otherwise appear identical at a single epoch.
\end{tipsblock}

\subsection{Growth and Expansion: What Cosmological Probes Measure} \label{sec:probes_overview}

Broadly speaking, cosmological probes constrain the $\Lambda$CDM model (and possible extensions to it) through two complementary observables: the \textbf{expansion history} of the Universe, encoded in the Hubble parameter $H(z)$, and the \textbf{growth history} of density perturbations, encoded in the linear growth factor $\delta(z)/\delta(\text{today})$. Both quantities depend on the matter density $\Omega_m$ and on the dark energy equation of state $w$, but with different sensitivities, so that a joint measurement of both breaks degeneracies that would otherwise persist if either were measured in isolation.

\smallskip

A wide range of independent techniques probe the Universe's expansion and growth history, each using different tracers and spanning various redshifts and systematics. These include primordial light-element abundances from Big Bang nucleosynthesis, CMB temperature and polarization anisotropies, the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest in quasar spectra, 21\,cm intensity mapping, the abundance and clustering of galaxy clusters, large-scale galaxy distribution, weak gravitational lensing, and standard candles like Type Ia supernovae. Major survey facilities such as Euclid, Rubin, SKAO, SPT, and eROSITA are all designed to exploit one or more of these probes, often covering multiple redshifts and wavelengths to maximize constraints on dark energy and test General Relativity.

\section{Standard Candles} \label{sec:standard_candles}

A \bfit{standard candle} is an astronomical object whose absolute magnitude (equivalently, intrinsic luminosity) is known, either because it is genuinely constant across the population or because it can be calibrated against some other observable property of the source. Classic examples include Cepheid and RR Lyrae variable stars and Type Ia supernovae. By measuring the apparent magnitude of a standard candle and comparing it to its known absolute magnitude, one obtains its distance; combining distance measurements across a range of redshifts (the \bfit{Hubble diagram}) then allows the expansion history, and hence the underlying cosmological parameters, to be inferred directly.

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsubsection{The Luminosity Distance} \label{subsec:luminosity_distance}

In an expanding Universe, objects of known luminosity appear dimmer than they would in Euclidean space, due to both redshifting and the spreading of photons over a larger area. This leads to the definition of the \textbf{luminosity distance} $D_L$ as
$$
f = \frac{L}{4\pi D_L^2} ,
$$
where $L$ is the bolometric luminosity of the source and $f$ its observed bolometric flux. 

To obtain an explicit expression for the luminosity distance $D_L$, we must consider three key effects in addition to the standard geometric dilution:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-Euclidean Geometry:} The Universe may have spatial curvature, so the flux from a source is distributed over an area determined by the comoving transverse distance $D_M$, rather than by a simple Euclidean separation.
    \item \textbf{Photon Redshifting:} As photons travel from source to observer, their energy is reduced by a factor of $1/(1+z)$ due to cosmological redshift. This directly decreases the observed flux.
    \item \textbf{Time Dilation:} The arrival rate of photons is further reduced by a factor of $1/(1+z)$, because the observer's clocks run faster than those in the rest frame of the emitting source.
\end{enumerate}

Taking all these factors into account, the observed flux becomes:
$$
f = \frac{L}{4\pi (1+z)^2 D_M^2} ,
$$
Comparing this result with the definition of $D_L$ shows that the general relation is $D_L = (1+z) D_M$.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.62\textwidth}
    For a spatially flat $\Lambda$CDM Universe, at redshifts well below matter-radiation equality ($z \ll z_{\text{eq}}$), the comoving distance reduces to a simple line-of-sight integral:
    $$
    D_L(z) = (1+z)\,D_M(z) = (1+z)\,\frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^z \frac{dz'}{E(z')}
    $$

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    where:
    $$
    E(z) \equiv \frac{H(z)}{H_0} = \sqrt{\Omega_m (1+z)^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}
    $$
    Because $E(z)$ depends on $\Omega_m$, $\Omega_\Lambda$, and $H(z)$, measuring the luminosity distance to standard candles at known redshifts is a powerful way to constrain these cosmological parameters.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.35\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/lum-dist.png}
    
    \captionof{figure}{Luminosity distance}
    \label{fig:luminosity_distance_models}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip


Each choice of parameters predicts a distinct $D_L(z)$ curve; these differences become especially pronounced for $z \gtrsim 1$, where the luminosity distances in matter-dominated and dark-energy-dominated models separate significantly.

\vspace{-0.5em}


\subsubsection{The Distance Modulus} \label{subsec:distance_modulus}

Observationally, source brightness is conventionally expressed in magnitudes rather than in flux directly. The \textbf{apparent magnitude} is defined as
$$
m = -2.5\log_{10}(f) + \text{const.} ,
$$
and the \textbf{absolute magnitude} $M$ is defined as the apparent magnitude the source would have if placed at a fiducial distance of $10\,\text{pc}$. 

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
    The difference between the two, the \textbf{distance modulus}, then encodes the luminosity distance directly:
    \medskip
    $$
    \mu \equiv m - M = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{D_L}{10\,\text{pc}}\right)
    $$
    Since $D_L(z)$ depends on the cosmological parameters as derived above, the distance modulus of a population of standard candles observed across a range of redshifts (the Hubble diagram $\mu(z)$) is one of the most direct empirical routes to constraining $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\Lambda$.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.37\textwidth}

\end{minipage}

\subsection{Cepheid Variables} \label{subsec:cepheids}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
\textbf{Cepheid variables} are pulsating stars whose pulsation period is tightly correlated with their intrinsic luminosity, the \bfit{period-luminosity relation} (or \textbf{Leavitt Law}, 1908). Because this relation allows the absolute magnitude of an individual Cepheid to be inferred directly from an easily measured observable, its pulsation period, Cepheids constitute the first standard candle used in astronomy, and remain a crucial rung of the local \bfit{cosmic distance ladder}: they are used to calibrate the absolute magnitude of Type Ia supernovae in nearby host galaxies, anchoring the entire SN Ia Hubble diagram to a physically calibrated distance scale.
\end{minipage}%

\subsection{Type Ia Supernovae} \label{subsec:sn1a}

The progenitor of a \textbf{Type Ia supernova} (SN Ia) is generally understood to be a white dwarf in a close binary system, accreting matter from its companion until it approaches the Chandrasekhar limit, $M_{\text{Ch}} \sim 1.4\,M_\odot$. Upon reaching this critical mass, the star is disrupted by an explosive thermonuclear burning event that synthesises iron-peak elements. Because essentially all SNe Ia explode at very nearly the same mass, the resulting population shows comparatively small intrinsic scatter in peak luminosity, making them attractive as standard candles.

\smallskip

SN Ia explosions are intrinsically rare, occurring at a rate of only ${\sim}\,10^{-4}\,[\text{yr\,Mpc}^3]^{-1}$ (roughly once per century in a galaxy like the Milky Way), but their peak luminosity is extraordinary, $M_V \simeq -19.3$, some $5\times10^9$ times brighter than the Sun and typically comparable to the integrated brightness of their entire host galaxy. This combination of rarity and extreme luminosity is precisely what makes them valuable cosmological probes: their brightness allows them to be detected out to $z > 1$, while their rarity is offset by monitoring very large volumes with dedicated time-domain surveys.

\subsubsection{Standardisation} \label{subsubsec:sn1a_standardization}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
Although SNe Ia show the highest photometric homogeneity of any supernova type, they are not, strictly speaking, standard candles: their peak absolute magnitudes show an intrinsic scatter of order $40\%$. The key empirical insight that rescues their use as precision distance indicators is that this scatter is not random, but correlates tightly with two independently measurable observables: the shape of the light curve (dimmer SNe decline more rapidly after maximum light, brighter SNe decline more slowly) and the SN colour at peak (redder events tend to be intrinsically fainter, largely due to dust extinction in the host galaxy).
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/sup-Ia.png}
    \captionof{figure}{SN Ia V-band light curves before (left) and after (right) stretch standardization.}
    \label{fig:sn1a_stretch}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

This motivates the \textbf{standardised distance modulus},
$$
\mu = m - M - \alpha\, x - \beta\, c ,
$$
where $x$ is the light-curve stretch parameter, $c$ is a measure of the SN colour, and $\alpha$, $\beta$ are empirically fitted nuisance parameters common to the entire SN sample. Once corrected in this way, the residual scatter in peak luminosity is reduced to the few-percent level, sufficient for precision cosmology.


\begin{minipage}{0.52\textwidth}
    \subsubsection{The Hubble Diagram} \label{subsubsec:sn1a_hubble_diagram}
    
Studying the evolution of the standardised distance modulus with redshift, the \textbf{Hubble diagram of SNe Ia}, provides a direct measurement of both $H_0$ (from the low-redshift slope) and the full expansion history $H(z)$ (from the shape of the curve at higher redshift, where the transition from decelerated to accelerated expansion becomes visible). The most recent, large SN Ia compilations (e.g.\ the DES-SN5YR sample of over 1\,600 supernovae) confirm this transition directly: residuals relative to a matter-only ($\Lambda = 0$) cosmology diverge systematically from zero at $z \lesssim 0.5$, exactly as expected once dark energy comes to dominate the energy budget.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.44\textwidth}

    \captionof{figure}{SNe Ia Hubble diagram.}

\end{minipage}

\subsection{$H_0$ and Low-Redshift Anchors}

Calibrating SN Ia distances at low redshift relies on independent geometric distance anchors, most commonly Cepheids in SN Ia host galaxies, themselves tied to precise parallax or maser measurements. This full calibration chain yields $H_0 = 73.2 \pm 1.3\,\text{km\,s}^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}^{-1}$ (\textbf{SH0ES}, Riess et al.\ 2021), a value in notable ($\sim4\sigma$) tension with that inferred from the CMB assuming $\Lambda$CDM.

\medskip

Recent \textbf{JWST} observations of Cepheid hosts previously observed with \textbf{HST} find tight ($\sim 1\sigma$) agreement, and alternative distance indicators like the tip of the red giant branch (TRGB) and J-region asymptotic giant branch (JAGB) also concur within uncertainty. This consistency across methods argues against a significant instrumental or Cepheid-specific systematic as the source of the discrepancy.

\section{Standard Clocks and Chronometers} \label{sec:cosmic_chronometers}

A complementary route to constraining the expansion history exploits the relation between cosmic time and redshift directly, rather than relying on a distance indicator. Since the age of the Universe at redshift $z$ is
$$
t(z) = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^z \frac{dz'}{E(z')(1+z')} ,
$$
finding the oldest objects at each redshift and using their inferred formation ages provides one route to constraining $H_0$ and the expansion history: such objects act as \textbf{standard clocks}, sources whose absolute age is independently known.

\smallskip

A more powerful variant of this idea avoids the need for absolute ages entirely. Rearranging the definition of the Hubble parameter,
$$
H(z) = -\frac{1}{1+z}\frac{dz}{dt} ,
$$
shows that a direct, model-independent measurement of $H(z)$ requires only the \textit{differential} age of the Universe between two nearby redshifts, that is, $dt$ for a given $dz$, rather than any absolute age. This motivates the search not for standard clocks, but for \textbf{standard chronometers}: a homogeneous population of objects with a synchronised formation epoch, whose internal clocks all began ``ticking'' at the same cosmic time, and which therefore trace the differential age evolution of the Universe in a consistent way regardless of the (unknown) absolute zero-point of that clock.

\subsection{The Cosmic Chronometers Method} \label{subsec:cc_method}

The \textbf{cosmic chronometers} (CC) method rests on two pillars: the selection of an optimal population of chronometers, and a sufficiently precise measurement of the differential age between redshift bins. Spectroscopic redshift surveys make the second requirement remarkably achievable, since a spectroscopic redshift typically constrains $\delta z/(1+z) \lesssim 10^{-3}$. The first requirement is met by \textbf{massive, passively evolving galaxies}: systems that formed the bulk (${>}\,90\%$) of their stellar mass in a single, rapid episode of star formation at high redshift ($z \gtrsim 2$-$3$), and subsequently exhausted their gas reservoir, evolving thereafter without further significant star formation. Because their star-formation histories are effectively synchronised and simple, the differential ageing of their stellar populations tracks the differential ageing of the Universe itself, with minimal dependence on the detailed astrophysics of any individual galaxy.

\subsection{The 4000 \AA\ Break} \label{subsec:d4000}

In practice, deriving an age from a galaxy spectrum requires adopting a model for stellar population synthesis, introducing a source of systematic uncertainty. This dependence can be minimised by working instead with a direct, empirically robust spectral feature: the \textbf{4000 \AA\ break} ($D_n4000$), a discontinuity in a galaxy's continuum flux around rest-frame $4000\,\text{\AA}$ produced by the accumulation of metal absorption lines in the atmospheres of older, cooler stars. 


\noindent
\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
Because the strength of this break grows monotonically with the age of a passively evolving stellar population (for a given star-formation history and metallicity), it provides an approximately linear proxy for stellar age:
$$
D_n4000 = A(\text{SFH}, Z/Z_\odot)\cdot \text{age} + B ,
$$
so that the differential age required by the cosmic chronometers method can be related directly to the differential $D_n4000$ measured between two redshift bins,
$$
H(z) = -\frac{1}{1+z}\, A(\text{SFH}, Z/Z_\odot)\, \frac{dz}{dD_n4000} .
$$
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{assets/4000-break.png}
    \captionof{figure}{4000 \AA\ break as age indicator, resulting $H(z)$ measurements \cite{Moresco2016}.}
    \label{fig:d4000}
\end{minipage}

Compiling cosmic-chronometer $H(z)$ measurements from a broad range of independent studies (spanning full-spectral-fitting and $D_n4000$-based methods alike) out to $z \sim 2$ yields a Hubble parameter history in good overall agreement with the flat $\Lambda$CDM model calibrated to Planck, and the resulting constraints in the $\Omega_m$-$H_0$ plane are highly complementary to, and consistent with, other late-time probes.

\subsection{Brightest Cluster Galaxies as Chronometers} \label{subsec:bcg_chronometers}

\bfit{Brightest cluster galaxies} (BCGs), the most massive and typically oldest galaxies in the Universe, sitting at the centres of massive galaxy clusters, constitute a particularly attractive chronometer population. Having formed their stars very early and very rapidly ($z > 2$), BCGs represent an unusually homogeneous and ``pure'' sample, reducing the risk of contamination from younger stellar populations that can otherwise bias the inferred age of a more heterogeneous galaxy sample. Applying the $D_n4000$ technique to a BCG sample yields an $H(z)$ measurement fully consistent with, and complementary to, the broader cosmic-chronometer compilation.

\begin{tipsblock}[Standard clocks and chronometers: strengths and limitations]
The cosmic chronometer method for measuring $H(z)$ features the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pros:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Provides a genuinely \textbf{model-independent} measurement of $H(z)$; does \emph{not} require any assumption about the underlying cosmological model.
    \item Highly \textbf{complementary} to geometric probes (BAO, SNe Ia, and the CMB), adding independent information.
    \item  \textbf{Valuable at intermediate redshifts} ($z \sim 0.1$--$2$), where other late-time probes are sparse. 
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Cons:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Requires \textbf{accurate age-dating} of the chronometer (galaxy) population.
    \item Must control for systematics from stellar population synthesis, star-formation history, and metallicity to avoid biasing $H(z)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{tipsblock}

\section{Big Bang Nucleosynthesis} \label{sec:bbn} 

\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}

\textbf{Big Bang nucleosynthesis} (BBN) refers to the synthesis of the light nuclides, principally deuterium (D), helium-3 ($^3\text{He}$), helium-4 ($^4\text{He}$), and lithium-7 ($^7\text{Li}$), during the first few minutes of the Universe's existence. Together with the CMB, BBN constitutes one of the two great pillars of quantitative evidence for the hot Big Bang model, and, crucially, probes cosmological conditions at a far earlier epoch, $t \sim 1$--$300\,\text{s}$, than any other observational method.

\subsection{Physics of BBN} \label{subsec:bbn_physics}

\subsubsection{Neutron-Proton Freeze-out} \label{subsubsec:np_freezeout}

At temperatures $k_BT \gg 1\,\text{MeV}$, the rate of the weak interactions coupling neutrons and protons, $\Gamma_{n\leftrightarrow p}$, greatly exceeds the cosmic expansion rate $H$, so that protons, neutrons, and electrons remain in chemical equilibrium via:
$$
e^+ + n \leftrightarrow p + \bar\nu_e , \qquad \nu_e + n \leftrightarrow p + e^- .
$$
As the Universe cools below $k_BT \sim 1\,\text{MeV}$ (corresponding to $t \sim 1\,\text{s}$), neutrinos decouple from the thermal plasma and the weak interaction rate falls below the expansion rate, $\Gamma_{n\leftrightarrow p} < H$: neutrons and protons cease to interconvert, and the neutron-to-proton ratio \bfit{freezes out}. At the freeze-out temperature, the equilibrium neutron abundance is suppressed relative to protons by the Boltzmann factor associated with their slightly larger mass, yielding $n/p \simeq 1/6$. Because free neutrons are unstable, with a mean lifetime $\tau_n = 890\,\text{s}$ comparable to the age of the Universe at this epoch, a further slow $\beta$-decay of the surviving free neutrons reduces the ratio to $n/p \simeq 1/7$ by the time nucleosynthesis proper begins.

\subsubsection{Formation of the Light Elements} \label{subsubsec:light_element_formation}

Protons and neutrons combine into $^4\text{He}$ through a short chain of two-body reactions:
$$
p + n \leftrightarrow {}^2\text{H} + \gamma , \qquad
{}^2\text{H} + n \to {}^3\text{H} + \gamma , \qquad
{}^3\text{H} + p \to {}^4\text{He} + \gamma ,
$$
$$
{}^2\text{H} + p \to {}^3\text{He} + \gamma , \qquad
{}^3\text{He} + n \to {}^4\text{He} + \gamma .
$$
The bottleneck of this entire chain is the formation of \textbf{deuterium}, the first nuclide produced: although deuterium begins forming as soon as $p + n$ collisions become energetically favourable, it is immediately photodissociated by the still-abundant population of energetic photons in the Wien tail of the blackbody spectrum, since even a tiny fraction of photons above the (comparatively low) deuterium binding energy vastly outnumber the baryons. 

\smallskip

Only once the number density of these dissociating photons, $n_\gamma^{\text{diss}}$, falls to a level comparable to the baryon number density $n_b$, at $k_BT \sim 0.1\,\text{MeV}$ ($T \sim 10^9\,\text{K}$, corresponding to $t_{\text{BBN}} \sim 150\,\text{s}$), does deuterium survive in appreciable quantity; this delay relative to the naive binding-energy expectation is known as the \textbf{deuterium bottleneck}. Once the bottleneck is overcome, the subsequent reactions proceed rapidly, and essentially all surviving free neutrons are incorporated into $^4\text{He}$ nuclei.

\medskip

Because the timing of the bottleneck, and hence the final light-element abundances, depends on the relative abundance of photons to baryons, the primordial helium mass fraction $Y_{\text{He}}$ is controlled by three physical inputs: the \textbf{freeze-out temperature} of the weak interactions (which itself depends on the total number of relativistic neutrino species present), the \textbf{mean neutron lifetime}, and the \textbf{baryon-to-photon ratio}:
$$
\eta \equiv \frac{n_b}{n_\gamma} = 2.7\times 10^{-8}\,\Omega_b h^2 ,
$$
which sets the epoch $t_{\text{BBN}}$ at which deuterium photodissociation becomes inefficient.

\medskip

Further reactions synthesise trace amounts of $^7\text{Li}$:
$$
{}^4\text{He} + {}^3\text{H} \leftrightarrow {}^7\text{Li} + \gamma , \qquad
{}^4\text{He} + {}^3\text{He} \leftrightarrow {}^7\text{Be} + \gamma , \qquad
{}^7\text{Be} + \gamma \leftrightarrow {}^7\text{Li} + p,
$$
with a relative abundance to hydrogen of only $\sim 10^{-9}$-$10^{-10}$. Nucleosynthesis effectively terminates here: no stable isotopes exist with mass number 5 or 8 ($^8\text{Be}$ is unstable), forming a nuclear gap that cannot be bridged at the low densities and temperatures characteristic of the BBN epoch, and the triple-alpha process responsible for producing $^{12}\text{C}$ in stellar interiors requires conditions far denser than those found in the early Universe. All heavier elements, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, iron, and beyond, are consequently produced later, through thermonuclear processes inside stars.

\subsection{Observational Signatures and Constraints} \label{subsec:bbn_observations}

\subsubsection{Primordial Abundances Determination} \label{subsubsec:primordial_abundances}

Each light nuclide offers a different observational window onto the primordial abundances, according to how strongly its post-BBN evolution is affected by subsequent stellar processing.

\medskip


\begin{minipage}{0.52\textwidth}
    \bfit{Deuterium} is the best probe of primordial baryon density: it is not produced after BBN, and any deuterium passing through stars is destroyed (converted to $^3\text{He}$). Its abundance therefore monotonically decreases over time, so any observed deuterium gives a firm lower limit on the primordial value. The most accurate primordial measurements come from absorption lines seen in a handful of high-redshift, metal-poor quasar sightlines, which are largely untouched by later stellar processing.
    
    \medskip
The evolution of $^3\text{He}$ after BBN is much more complicated, involving both stellar production and destruction; as a result, observations are limited to our Solar System and Galactic HII regions. In contrast, $^4\text{He}$ is more straightforward: stars continually convert hydrogen to helium over time, so the observed $^4\text{He}$ abundance today is always at least the primordial value. The key measurements come from hydrogen and helium recombination lines in very metal-poor extragalactic HII regions, chosen to minimize stellar processing effects. 
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/abundances.png}
    \captionof{figure}{BBN predictions for the primordial abundances \cite{Fields}.}
    \label{fig:bbn_abundances}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Finally, $^7\text{Li}$ observed today is affected by continuous production in stars and cosmic-ray interactions, so its primordial abundance must be estimated from the oldest, most metal-poor halo stars, where subsequent enrichment is minimal.

\begin{observationblock}[The $^7$Li problem]
Standard BBN, using the CMB-measured baryon density, \textbf{overestimates} the primordial $^7\text{Li}$ abundance by about a factor of 3 compared to observations of old, metal-poor (Population II) halo stars:
$$
\left(\frac{^7\text{Li}}{\text{H}}\right)_{\text{BBN}} \sim 5\times10^{-10} , \qquad
\left(\frac{^7\text{Li}}{\text{H}}\right)_{\text{obs}} \sim 1.6\times10^{-10} .
$$
This ongoing disagreement is the well-known \bfit{lithium problem}. Two main explanations have been proposed, but neither fully resolves it. One possibility is lithium depletion in old stars through stellar processes (like diffusion, mixing, or destruction), yet no model accounts for the consistently low lithium found in metal-poor halo stars. The other is that nuclear reaction rates, especially those for $^7\text{Be}$ destruction, have been underestimated, but updated lab measurements have not solved the issue.
\end{observationblock}

\subsubsection{Effective Number of Relativistic Species} \label{subsubsec:neff}

Besides determining light-element abundances, BBN and the CMB also constrain the early Universe's total radiation content via the \bfit{effective number of relativistic species}, $N_{\text{eff}}$. This parameter measures the energy density contributed by relativistic particles other than photons, mainly the three known neutrino types, but also any possible new, light, weakly interacting species:
$$
\rho_{\text{rad}} = \left[1 + \frac{7}{8}\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{4/3} N_{\text{eff}}\right] \rho_\gamma .
$$
The Standard Model, allowing for small neutrino heating from $e^+e^-$ annihilation, predicts $N_{\text{eff}} = 3.044$. Any new relativistic particles would increase $N_{\text{eff}}$, raising the radiation density at a fixed photon temperature. This speeds up the cosmic expansion during BBN and recombination (as $H \propto \sqrt{\rho_{\text{rad}}}$), affecting both primordial element abundances and the pattern of CMB acoustic peaks. Combined analyses of the CMB, BAO, and primordial abundance data (including recent deuterium and helium measurements from extremely metal-poor regions) currently find $N_{\text{eff}}$ in excellent agreement with the Standard Model, placing strong limits on the existence of new light particles.

\section{Cluster Number Counts} \label{sec:cluster_number_counts}

Beyond their internal astrophysics (\cref{ch:icm}), galaxy clusters are a valuable cosmological probe: as the most massive peaks in the matter density field, their abundance as a function of mass and redshift is highly sensitive to both the growth of structure and the Universe's expansion history.

\subsubsection{Galaxy Clusters as a Cosmological Probe} \label{subsubsec:clusters_cosmo_probe}

The number and distribution of galaxy clusters depend mainly on two factors: the \bfit{growth of structure}, described by the fluctuation amplitude $\sigma_8$ (the root-mean-square of the density field on $8\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}$ scales), and the \bfit{expansion history} set by $\Omega_m$. Because these effects are partly degenerate in cluster counts, and weak lensing mainly constrains their combination,
\smallskip
$$
S_8 \equiv \sigma_8 \left(\Omega_m / 0.3\right)^{0.5}
$$
$S_8$ has become the standard parameter for comparing late-time measurements of structure growth (from clusters and lensing) to early-Universe (CMB) predictions. Cluster abundance is also sensitive to the dark energy equation-of-state $w$, the total neutrino mass, and possible deviations from General Relativity, making cluster counts a powerful and versatile cosmological probe.


\subsection{Structure Formation and the Spherical Collapse Model} \label{subsec:spherical_collapse}

As introduced in \cref{subsec:structure_formation}, structure grows hierarchically in the $\Lambda$CDM scenario: small overdensities are the first to decouple from the cosmic expansion and collapse, and the resulting \textbf{dark matter halos} subsequently merge into progressively more massive systems, culminating in the galaxy clusters that occupy the knots of the cosmic web.

\smallskip

A simple but remarkably useful analytic tool for understanding when and where these halos form is the \textbf{spherical collapse model}, which follows the evolution of an isolated, spherically symmetric density perturbation embedded in an otherwise homogeneous, expanding background.

\smallskip

\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{assets/spherical-collapse.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left:} Stages of spherical collapse: initial expansion, turnaround, and final virializaion. \textbf{Right:} Evolution of the overdense region's density compared to the background density as a function of scale factor, highlighting turnaround, collapse, virialisation, and key overdensity milestones.}
\end{center}

\smallskip

At first, the overdense region expands together with the Universe: the mean background density evolves as $\bar\rho_{\text{bkg}} \propto a^{-3}$. However, because the perturbation is denser than its surroundings, it expands more slowly. Eventually, it reaches a key stage called \textbf{turnaround}, defined by $\dot a = 0$: here, the overdensity stops expanding, detaches from the overall Hubble flow, and starts to collapse under its own gravity. In an idealized scenario (perfectly spherical, no pressure), this collapse would produce a singularity. In reality, as material falls in, outer mass shells overtake inner ones (\textbf{shell crossing}), mixing orbits and causing the system to \textbf{virialise} into a stable, gravitationally bound halo.

\smallskip

Applying the scalar virial theorem ($2K + U = 0$) reveals that the final, virialised halo has a radius half that at turnaround: $R(a_{\text{vir}}) = R(a_{\text{max}})/2$. As a result, its density increases by a further factor of $2^3 = 8$ beyond the turnaround stage. 

\smallskip
Although the spherical collapse model breaks down after shell crossing, it still gives reliable estimates for key thresholds in the \emph{linearly} evolved density field:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The critical, linearly extrapolated \textbf{density threshold} for collapse is $\delta_{\text{lin}} = \delta_c \simeq 1.686$ (nearly invariant for Einstein-de Sitter cosmologies).
    \item Virialised halos are expected to have a mean \textbf{overdensity} of $\Delta_{\text{vir}} \simeq 178$ times the background density at the time of collapse.
\end{itemize}

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.52\textwidth}
The value $\delta_c$ provides the collapse criterion for the full, three-dimensional density field: at any point $\vec{x}$ and time $t$, a region is expected to have collapsed into a halo once its linearly extrapolated density contrast exceeds $\delta_c$. Assuming the initial density field is Gaussian, the fraction of cosmic volume satisfying $\delta(\vec x, t) > \delta_c$ depends only on the variance of the (smoothed) density field, $\sigma^2 = \langle \delta^2 \rangle$: this statistic thus directly connects primordial density fluctuations to the abundance of collapsed halos, forming the basis of the halo mass function below.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/delta_c.png}
    \captionof{figure}{1D density field with peaks above threshold $\delta_c$ marked as forming halos}
\end{minipage}

\subsection{The Halo Mass Function} \label{subsec:halo_mass_function}

The observed abundance of clusters, differential in redshift and solid angle, factorises into a purely geometric term and a term encoding the growth of structure:

$$
\frac{dN}{dz\,d\Omega} = \underbrace{\frac{dV}{dz\,d\Omega}}_{\text{geometry}}\;\underbrace{n(M,z)}_{\text{growth}} ,
$$
where $dV/(dz\,d\Omega)$ is the comoving volume element (itself a function of $H(z)$ and hence of the expansion history) and $n(M,z)$ is the \bfit{halo mass function}: the comoving number density of halos per unit mass at redshift $z$.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
Extending the spherical collapse argument of \cref{subsec:spherical_collapse}, the halo mass function is written as
$$
n(z,M) = \frac{\rho_m}{M}\,f(\sigma)\,\frac{d\ln(\sigma^{-1})}{dM} ,
$$
where $\rho_m/M$ converts a comoving number density of collapsed regions into a number density of halos of mass $M$, and $f(\sigma)$, the \textbf{multiplicity function}, encodes the fraction of mass collapsed into halos as a function of the variance $\sigma$ evaluated on the mass scale $M$. The variance is obtained from the matter power spectrum,
$$
\sigma(z,R) = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\int_0^\infty dk\,k^2\,P_m(z,k)\,|W(kR)|^2 ,
$$
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.39\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/hmf-vs-mass.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Halo mass function (HMF) vs.~mass at $z=0$ for different values of $\Omega_m$, $\sigma_8$, and $w$}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

with $W(kR)$ a window function (typically a top-hat) smoothing the density field on the comoving scale $R$ corresponding to mass $M$.

\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
    The abundance of galaxy clusters as a function of redshift and mass offers key insights into both structure growth and cosmic expansion. The comoving volume element reflects how observable volume changes with redshift, while the halo mass function tracks the buildup of structure over time. Since the halo mass function falls off exponentially at high masses (as shown in the logarithmic plots), and is highly sensitive to cosmological parameters, cluster counts serve as a powerful probe of cosmology.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{assets/cluster-counts.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Cluster counts vs redshift.}
\end{minipage}

\subsubsection{Massive Neutrinos} \label{subsubsec:hmf_neutrinos}

\textbf{Massive neutrinos} have a distinctive impact on cosmic structure formation. Their high thermal velocities prevent them from clustering on small (galaxy-sized) scales, reducing the number of galaxy clusters that can form. In the early Universe, neutrinos are produced in thermal equilibrium and decouple from the plasma at temperatures around $T_{\text{dec}} \sim 2$-$4\,\text{MeV}$. At decoupling, sub-eV neutrinos are still highly relativistic, contributing to the energy density as \textit{radiation} and moving (“free-streaming”) nearly at the speed of light, with a typical free-streaming length $\lambda_{\text{fs}} \sim c/H \sim ct$. As the Universe expands and cools, each neutrino species eventually becomes non-relativistic at a redshift
$$
1 + z_{\text{nr}} = \frac{1}{a_{\text{nr}}} \simeq 2\times10^3 \left(\frac{m_\nu}{1\,\text{eV}}\right) ,
$$
after which massive neutrinos contribute to the \textit{matter} budget. The free-streaming wavenumber is:
$$
k_{\text{nr}} = 2\pi\,\frac{a_{\text{nr}}}{\lambda_{\text{fs}}} \simeq 0.018\,\Omega_m^{1/2}\left(\frac{m_\nu}{1\,\text{eV}}\right) h\,\text{Mpc}^{-1} ,
$$
marks the transition, in $k$-space, between a super-horizon regime (where perturbations remain frozen) and a growth regime accessible to structure formation.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.36\textwidth]{assets/free-evo.png}
    \hspace{1em}
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{assets/neutrino-mf.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left}: free-streaming wavenumber $k_{\text{fs}}$ vs.\ scale factor $a$, showing transition regimes. \textbf{Right}: suppression of the halo mass function for increasing total neutrino mass $\sum m_\nu$.}
\end{center}

\medskip

Because free-streaming neutrinos remain diffuse and effectively unclustered on scales below $\lambda_{\text{fs}}$, they contribute a smooth background that does not participate in gravitational collapse. Massive neutrinos therefore leave two imprints on structure formation: they \textbf{delay the epoch of matter-radiation equality} (since a larger radiation-like component at early times pushes equality to lower redshift), and they \textbf{suppress the growth of density fluctuations} on scales smaller than the free-streaming length. The net effect is a suppression of the halo mass function that grows with the total neutrino mass $\sum m_\nu$ and is most pronounced at the high-mass end, making cluster counts, together with the CMB and large-scale structure, one of the most sensitive cosmological probes of $\sum m_\nu$.

\subsubsection{Modified Gravity} \label{subsubsec:hmf_modgrav}

An alternative to a dark energy component is to modify the gravitational action itself. A widely studied class of such models is $f(R)$ gravity, in which the Einstein-Hilbert action is generalised to

$$
S = \frac{1}{16\pi G}\int \sqrt{-g}\,\bigl[R + f(R)\bigr]\,d^4x .
$$

Suitably chosen functions $f(R)$ can drive accelerated expansion without an explicit cosmological constant, while simultaneously enhancing the effective strength of gravity on cosmological scales, which in turn boosts the growth rate of structure and enhances the halo mass function relative to $\Lambda$CDM, particularly at the massive end and at low redshift. Because unscreened enhanced gravity would violate stringent Solar System tests of GR, viable $f(R)$ models incorporate a \bfit{screening mechanism} (the chameleon mechanism) that restores standard GR in high-density environments, such as the interior of massive halos, while allowing modified dynamics to operate in the low-density cosmic web; this environment dependence is itself a distinctive observational signature that halo-mass-function measurements can, in principle, test.

\subsubsection{The Multiplicity Function and its universality} \label{subsubsec:multiplicity_function}

The functional form of $f(\sigma)$ was first derived analytically by Press \& Schechter (1974) from the spherical collapse of a Gaussian density field, and later refined by Sheth \& Tormen (1999) using an ellipsoidal collapse model, which better captures tidal deformations of collapsing regions. Modern studies of $f(\sigma)$, however, are calibrated directly against large suites of N-body simulations, which capture the full non-linear collapse dynamics that analytic models can only approximate.

\smallskip

The multiplicity function is often described as "\bfit{universal}", in the sense that a single functional form of $f(\sigma)$ is assumed to hold across cosmologies, redshifts, and halo definitions. In practice this universality is only approximate, and its calibration depends sensitively on several choices: the specifics of the N-body simulation used (box size, particle number, force softening), the halo-finding algorithm (e.g.\ friends-of-friends or spherical-overdensity finders), the adopted mass definition (e.g.\ $M_{200,m}$ or $M_{500,c}$), and the cosmological model itself (massive neutrinos and modified gravity both induce departures from a fixed $\Lambda$CDM model). Precision cluster cosmology therefore requires recalibrating, or validating, $f(\sigma)$ for the specific simulation pipeline, mass definition, and cosmological model being tested.

\subsubsection{Baryonic Effects} \label{subsubsec:hmf_baryons}

\begin{minipage}{0.44\textwidth}

Dark-matter-only simulations miss crucial processes: \bfit{baryonic feedback}, from radiative cooling, star formation, supernovae, and AGN feedback, can redistribute or expel gas from halo centres, altering the total mass assigned by standard halo finders. These baryonic effects are most pronounced in halo cores and low-mass systems, but even cluster-scale halos can be affected. For accurate cosmological results, halo mass function calibrations must rely on hydrodynamical simulations including baryonic physics, or explicitly correct for baryonic effects as a function of mass and radius.
\end{minipage}%

\section{From Theory to Observation} \label{sec:theory_to_observation}


\subsection{Observable-Mass Relations and Selection Functions} \label{subsec:observable_mass}

Halo mass is not itself an observable quantity: cluster surveys instead detect and characterise clusters through some observable proxy $\mathcal{O}$ (richness, X-ray luminosity or photon counts, SZ signal), whose connection to the halo mass function prediction $n(M,z)$ must be modelled explicitly before a comparison to theory is possible.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}

Because individual, high-precision mass measurements are expensive and infeasible for entire cluster samples, cosmological analyses instead rely on an \textbf{observable-mass relation}, $P(\mathcal{O}\mid M)$, calibrated on a smaller, well-characterised sub-sample with independent mass estimates (typically from weak lensing).
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.57\textwidth}
    \vspace{-1em}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/mass-estimate.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Cluster mass must be inferred by combining multiple observable proxies}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

A second essential ingredient is the survey's \textbf{selection function}: the probability that a cluster of a given true mass is actually detected and included in the catalogue, which in flux- or S/N-limited surveys is generally a smoothly increasing function of mass rather than a sharp cut. Because cosmological and observable-mass relation parameters are highly degenerate in the observed counts, robust cluster cosmology requires \textit{jointly} fitting cluster abundance and independent mass calibration data (typically weak-lensing estimates), to constrain both cosmology and the observable-mass relation together.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.47\textwidth}
    A shallower or steeper slope, or a higher or lower amplitude, in the observable-mass relation shifts the inferred abundance-cosmology constraints along essentially the same degeneracy direction in the $(\Omega_m, \sigma_8)$ plane as a genuine change in cosmological parameters. In practice, uncertain mass calibration is the leading systematic that constrains how tightly current cluster cosmology can be limited.
\end{minipage}%

\subsection{Cluster Detection Techniques} \label{subsec:cluster_detection}

Different observational windows probe different cluster components (\cref{subsec:galaxy_clusters}) and consequently define distinct detection techniques, mass proxies, and selection functions.

\subsubsection{Photometric Surveys} \label{subsubsec:photometric_detection}

Optical and NIR surveys detect clusters as overdensities of red-sequence galaxies (passive, uniformly red member galaxies occupying a tight colour-magnitude relation) or through their weak-lensing signal. The principal mass proxy is the \textbf{richness} $\lambda$, an estimate of the number of red-sequence member galaxies above some luminosity threshold; luminosity, lensing signal, and (where spectra are available) velocity dispersion provide complementary or cross-calibrating proxies. Photometric, richness-selected catalogues such as the \textbf{redMaPPer} algorithm applied to the Dark Energy Survey reach considerably lower mass limits than ICM-selected samples (see \cref{subsubsec:cluster_catalogs_multi}), at the cost of a selection function that is intrinsically harder to model, since richness is more susceptible to projection effects and to the details of galaxy evolution than either X-ray or SZ observables.


\subsubsection{X-ray Surveys} \label{subsubsec:xray_detection}

Building on the bremsstrahlung physics introduced in \cref{subsec:icm_spectrum}, X-ray surveys detect clusters as extended, luminous X-ray sources, with mass proxies including the X-ray luminosity $L_X$, the spectroscopic temperature $T_X$, and the combined thermal energy proxy $Y_X \equiv M_{\text{gas}}\,T_X$. Because the X-ray emissivity scales as $n_e^2$ (\cref{subsec:icm_spectrum}), X-ray surveys are comparatively insensitive to line-of-sight projection effects (which mainly wash out the sharp $n_e^2$ contrast, rather than mimicking a spurious detection): a genuine advantage over richness-based selection, though it comes at the cost of the steep $(1+z)^{-4}$ surface-brightness dimming already discussed in \cref{subsubsec:icm_sb}, which limits the depth of flux-limited X-ray cluster samples at high redshift.

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsubsection{SZ Surveys} \label{subsubsec:sz_detection}

Exploiting the thermal SZ effect introduced in \cref{subsec:tsz}, millimetre surveys detect clusters via matched filtering of the Compton-$y$ signal (\cref{subsubsec:tsz_secondary} and, in more detail, \cref{subsec:sz_catalogs}). Because the SZ amplitude is, to good approximation, independent of redshift (\cref{subsec:tsz}), SZ-selected catalogues probe massive clusters out to considerably higher redshift than flux-limited X-ray samples for a comparable exposure, at a selection threshold set essentially by mass alone.

\vspace{-0.5em}


\subsubsection{Cluster Catalogues Across Wavelengths} \label{subsubsec:cluster_catalogs_multi}

\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
\textbf{ICM-selected catalogues} (X-ray/SZ surveys) detect clusters above $M \sim 2\times10^{14}\,M_\odot$, offering a clean selection function—especially for SZ, which is redshift independent. However, optical or NIR follow-up is needed for redshift confirmation, spectroscopic identification, and lensing-based mass calibration.

\textbf{Optically selected catalogues}, in contrast, reach lower masses ($M \sim 5\times10^{13}\,M_\odot$) and yield about ten times more clusters, with weak-lensing and photometric-redshift data readily available. However, their selection function is harder to model due to increased sensitivity to projection and galaxy evolution effects.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.47\textwidth}
    \vspace{-2em}
    \centering

\subsection{Cluster Mass Measurements} \label{subsec:cluster_mass_measurements}

\subsubsection{X-ray Hydrostatic Masses} \label{subsubsec:xray_mass_recap}

The hydrostatic mass estimator was already derived in \cref{subsec:hse}; for dynamically relaxed clusters, comparisons against simulations indicate a hydrostatic-to-true mass ratio $M_{\text{HE}}/M_{\text{true}} \sim 0.85$, with a scatter of order $10\%$, consistent with the hydrostatic mass bias discussed there.

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsubsection{Dynamical Masses from Spectroscopy} \label{subsubsec:dynamical_mass}

An independent mass estimate follows from the kinematics of the cluster's galaxy population itself. Assuming spherical symmetry and dynamical equilibrium, the \textbf{Jeans equation} relates the enclosed mass to the radial velocity dispersion profile $\sigma_r(r)$ and the number density profile $n_{\text{gal}}(r)$ of a tracer population (here, member galaxies):
$$
M(<r) = -\frac{r\,\sigma_r^2}{G}\left(\frac{d\ln \sigma_r^2}{d\ln r} + \frac{d\ln n_{\text{gal}}}{d\ln r} + 2\beta\right)
$$
where $\beta$ is the velocity anisotropy parameter, quantifying any preference for radial versus tangential orbits among the tracer population.


Because line-of-sight velocities alone cannot separate radial and tangential motions, $\beta$ is typically either assumed or constrained jointly with the mass profile using extended spectroscopic samples.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
    A complementary technique, the \textbf{caustic method}, uses the full projected phase-space distribution (line-of-sight velocity vs. projected radius) of galaxies around a cluster. The escape velocity as a function of radius forms a characteristic "trumpet"-shaped caustic that ideally separates bound members from interlopers, allowing the cluster mass profile to be reconstructed without assuming dynamical equilibrium as in the Jeans method. In practice, however, real survey (e.g. Euclid) are much noisier: contaminants can occupy the same phase-space regions as cluster members, and low number statistics further complicate the analysis. As a result, applying the caustic method robustly is challenging.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.41\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/caustic.png}
    
    \captionof{figure}{Caustic diagram: line-of-sight velocity versus projected radius.}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

Both techniques fundamentally depend on accurate membership identification, as contamination from interloping galaxies (objects not physically bound to the cluster) remains one of the principal sources of systematic uncertainty in mass estimates.



\begin{minipage}{0.57\textwidth}
    \subsubsection{Masses from Gravitational Lensing} \label{subsubsec:lensing_mass}

Gravitational lensing offers what is often regarded as the most direct cluster mass estimator, since the deflection of background light depends only on the total projected mass along the line of sight and not on the cluster's dynamical state. Two regimes are relevant. In the cluster core ($\sim$ a few hundred kpc), the deflection can be strong enough to produce multiple images or extended arcs of a single background source (\textbf{strong lensing}), enabling precise measurements of the projected mass enclosed by the arcs, though the reconstruction depends on assumptions about the lens geometry and mass model, introducing systematic uncertainties of order $10$-$20\%$.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.87\textwidth]{assets/strong-lensing.png}

    \vspace{-1em}


    \captionof{figure}{Strong-lensing arcs and surface-mass-density in a cluster core.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

At larger radii ($R \gtrsim 300\,\text{kpc}$), the cluster's tidal field instead produces a subtle, coherent distortion in the shapes of background galaxies, the \textbf{weak lensing} regime.

\smallskip
 
\begin{minipage}{0.57\textwidth}

Measuring the mean tangential shear profile $\gamma_t(R)$ around the cluster allows the projected surface mass density $\Sigma(R)$ to be reconstructed,
$$
\Sigma(R) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} d\chi\;\Delta\rho\!\left(\sqrt{R^2+\chi^2}\right) ,
$$
though converting this into a three-dimensional mass profile requires assuming a parametric halo density model (e.g.\ NFW or Einasto, \cref{subsec:hmf_baryons_profile}) together with a term describing correlated structure along the line of sight (the "2-halo" term). Because the weak-lensing signal of an individual cluster is noisy, wide-field surveys typically stack the shear of many clusters at fixed observable to calibrate the mean observable-mass relation, reducing shape noise and projection systematics.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.87\textwidth]{assets/weak-lensing.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left}: Weak lensing effect. \textbf{Bottom} measured stacked tangential shear profile.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

    A comparison of mass profiles reconstructed from these independent techniques, dynamical (Jeans and caustic), weak lensing, and strong lensing, generally shows \bfit{good mutual consistency} across the radial ranges where they overlap, lending confidence to the combined multi-technique mass profile as a benchmark for validating X-ray hydrostatic masses.

\subsubsection{CMB Cluster Lensing} \label{subsubsec:cmb_cluster_lensing}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
A further, largely independent lensing channel exploits the CMB itself as the background source at $z\approx 1100$. As CMB photons traverse a foreground cluster, they are gravitationally deflected by its potential well; because the CMB temperature field carries a local gradient (from the primary anisotropies), this deflection locally shifts the observed gradient, imprinting a characteristic \bfit{dipole-like distortion} in the temperature (or polarisation) map centred on the cluster, with an amplitude proportional to the cluster mass and to the magnitude of the local CMB gradient.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.41\textwidth}
    \centering

The induced distortion is tiny, of order $10\,\mu\text{K}$ for a $10^{15}\,M_\odot$ halo, well below the noise level of any individual cluster measurement, but it can be recovered statistically by stacking the CMB signal around large samples of optically or SZ-selected clusters; applying this technique to thousands of redMaPPer clusters yields a robust detection of the stacked convergence profile.

\begin{minipage}{0.57\textwidth}

SPT-Pol data, for example, have already achieved a $4.8\sigma$ detection of the polarisation lensing dipole. Since this method uses the CMB as a background at a fixed and very high redshift, it provides a cluster mass calibration that is systematically independent from optical weak-lensing measurements.

\subsection{Scaling Relations and Multi-Wavelength Correlations} \label{subsec:scaling_relations}

Different detection techniques imply different mass proxies, different calibration datasets, and different systematics; a rigorous calibration of the observable-mass relation(s) therefore requires, jointly: a well-defined selection function, a model for the parent halo population as a function of mass (the halo mass function itself), and a model for the full probability distribution of the multivariate observable space, $P(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{O}\mid M)$, where $\mathcal{X}$ denotes a low-scatter mass proxy (e.g.\ from lensing) used for calibration and $\mathcal{O}$ the primary detection observable.

\medskip

Several statistical effects complicate a naive fit of the observable-mass relation to real data:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Malmquist bias} arises because a flux- or signal-to-noise-limited sample preferentially detects the intrinsically brighter (or higher-signal) objects at a given mass, biasing a naive fit toward higher observable amplitudes near the detection threshold.
    \item \textbf{Eddington bias} is the related effect whereby, because the mass function falls steeply with mass, scatter in the observable-mass relation preferentially up-scatters more numerous low-mass objects into a high-observable bin, again biasing naive fits.
    \item When several observables are measured for the same cluster (e.g.\ richness, X-ray luminosity, and SZ signal), their scatter about the mean observable-mass relation is generally \textbf{correlated}, since a single physical property of the cluster (such as its dynamical state or orientation) can simultaneously boost or suppress several observables at once; ignoring this correlated scatter biases both the mass calibration and the resulting cosmological constraints.
\end{itemize}

All of these effects must be forward-modelled, rather than corrected for post hoc, within a joint cosmology plus scaling-relation analysis.

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.46\textwidth]{assets/scaling-relations.png}
    \hspace{2em}
    \includegraphics[width=0.37\textwidth]{assets/correlation.png}
    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left}: Malmquist bias, Eddington bias, and correlated scatter in observable-mass relations. \textbf{Right}: Correlation matrix and scatter plot of log-residuals for 2D observables.}
\end{center}

\smallskip

A closely related complication is that cluster observables are measured in \textit{projection}: any single sightline captures only the two-dimensional projected mass or emission along a cylinder through the cluster, rather than the intrinsic three-dimensional quantity. Comparisons between projected and de-projected quantities in simulated cluster catalogues show that line-of-sight projections systematically increase the scatter and skewness of observable-mass relations, and, because a given large-scale-structure filament along the line of sight can simultaneously boost several observables, they introduce additional correlations between observables measured at different wavelengths beyond those present in the fully three-dimensional (de-projected) observable space.

Recent cosmological studies using galaxy clusters (X-ray, SZ, and optical) provide consistent measurements of key parameters such as $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$.

This difference  (often attributed to systematics like cluster mass calibration) remains smaller than current uncertainties. Combining cluster abundances with CMB or lensing data helps break parameter degeneracies and delivers tighter, more robust cosmological constraints.

\section{Other Cosmological Tests with Galaxy Clusters} \label{sec:other_cluster_tests}

Beyond cluster abundance, several further cosmological and fundamental-physics tests exploit individual or stacked cluster observations directly.

\subsection{The Gas Mass Fraction} \label{subsec:gas_mass_fraction}

Because massive, dynamically relaxed clusters are thought to retain a representative sample of the Universe's baryon and dark matter content, their \bfit{gas mass fraction}, $f_{\text{gas}} \equiv M_{\text{gas}}/M_{\text{tot}}$, should approximate the cosmic baryon fraction $\Omega_b/\Omega_m$. 

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}

The gas mass fraction, $f_{\text{gas}} = M_{\text{gas}} / M_{\text{tot}}$, measured in massive, dynamically relaxed clusters, is a valuable cosmological probe. Both $M_{\text{gas}}$ and $M_{\text{tot}}$ are inferred from X-ray data, with their determination depending on the assumed cosmology, primarily through the angular diameter distance relating flux and luminosity:
$$
f_{\text{gas}} \propto \left(\frac{\Omega_b}{\Omega_m}\right)\left[\frac{d^{\text{ref}}(z)}{d(z)}\right]^{3/2}
$$
where $d^{\mathrm{ref}}(z)$ is the reference (assumed) luminosity distance, and $d(z)$ is the true luminosity distance. In the correct cosmology, $f_{\text{gas}}$ remains constant with redshift ($d^{\text{ref}}(z)/d(z) = 1$), while deviations from this indicate an incorrect cosmological model.

\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/gas-mass.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Gas mass fraction $f_{\rm gas}$ vs.\ redshift for clusters.}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

At low redshift ($z \lesssim 0.15$), precise $f_{\text{gas}}$ measurements in clusters, combined with external priors on $\Omega_b h^2$ (mainly) and $h$, provide strong constraints on $\Omega_m$:
$$
f_{\text{gas}}(z \lesssim 0.15) \propto \frac{\Omega_b}{\Omega_m} h^{3/2}
$$
A nearly flat $f_{\text{gas}}$ at low $z$ therefore selects a specific combination of $\Omega_m$ and $h$, making it a sensitive cosmological tool. At higher redshift, the dependence on angular diameter distance causes $f_{\text{gas}}(z)$ to evolve if an incorrect cosmology is adopted:
$$
f_{\text{gas}}(z) \propto d(z)^{-3/2}
$$
Maintaining a nearly constant $f_{\text{gas}}(z)$ with redshift thus acts as a geometric test, complementary to those based on cluster abundance.

\subsubsection{Systematics}

The main systematics affecting this test include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uncertainties in mass estimates:} Errors in the total mass determination, especially due to hydrostatic bias, which arises if the intracluster gas is not perfectly supported by thermal pressure.
    \item \textbf{Selection effects:} Only dynamically relaxed clusters are selected to ensure the validity of hydrostatic equilibrium, which introduces a bias since these clusters may not be representative.
    \item \textbf{Aperture dependence:} The inferred gas mass fraction $f_{\mathrm{gas}}$ changes with the radius or aperture selected for the measurement.
    \item \textbf{Deviation from the universal baryon fraction:} The measured $f_{\mathrm{gas}}$ is typically lower than the cosmic baryon fraction due to baryon depletion processes; this depletion factor $Y_B(z)$ must be calibrated from hydrodynamical simulations.
\end{itemize}

\newpage
\subsection{$H_0$ from X-ray and SZ Distance Measurements} \label{subsec:h0_xsz}

\begin{minipage}{0.68\textwidth}
A geometric route to the Hubble constant follows from combining the cluster's X-ray emission with its SZ signal. Both observables depend on the same underlying electron density $n_e$ along the line of sight, but with different powers (\cref{subsec:tsz}): 
$$
\begin{cases}
    E \propto \int n_e^2\,dl \\
    A \propto \int n_e\,dl
\end{cases}
$$

This distinct scaling allows the two observables to be combined into a quantity with dimensions of length, $A^2/E \simeq \Delta L$, independent of any assumed distance; comparing this physical length scale to the cluster's observed angular size $\theta$ then yields a purely geometric estimate of the angular diameter distance,
$$
D_A(z) = \frac{A^2}{E\,\theta} ,
$$
entirely independent of the local distance ladder.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.3\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{assets/X-SZ-dist.png}
    \captionof{figure}{X-ray/SZ combined into a standard-ruler}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Combining X-ray and SZ measurements in samples of relaxed clusters across redshift provides a geometric route to $H_0$, but in practice, these estimates carry large uncertainties and show considerable scatter, typically clustering around $60$-$75\,\text{km/s/Mpc}$ and agreeing broadly with other methods. The main limitation is the rarity of clusters with sufficiently high-quality, relaxed systems. In recent years, error bars have often increased, as more sources of systematic uncertainty are identified and treated more conservatively. This reflects a general trend: as understanding improves, recognizing greater astrophysical complexities often leads to larger, not smaller, reported uncertainties.

\bigskip

\begin{minipage}{0.59\textwidth}
\subsection{Halo Density Profiles} \label{subsec:hmf_baryons_profile}

Collisionless N-body and hydrodynamical simulations of $\Lambda$CDM structure formation robustly predict a characteristic, quasi-universal radial density profile for dark matter halos. The \textbf{Navarro-Frenk-White} (NFW) profile,
$$
\rho_{\text{NFW}}(r) = \frac{\rho_s}{(r/r_s)\,(1+r/r_s)^2} ,
$$
features an inner \bfit{cusp}, $\rho \propto r^{-1}$, steepening to $\rho \propto r^{-3}$ at large radii, with a transition scale $r_s$; the \bfit{Einasto} profile provides an alternative, slightly better-fitting parametrisation without a sharp asymptotic power-law cusp.

\medskip

The density profile can also be described in terms of its \textbf{concentration parameter}, defined as $c_{\text{NFW}} \equiv r_{200} / r_s$. The concentration reflects how centrally peaked a halo is, and it primarily depends on the halo's mass and redshift: lower-mass and higher-redshift halos tend to be more concentrated because they formed earlier, when the universe was denser. Additional scatter in the concentration-mass relation arises from differences in individual halo formation histories, degrees of dynamical relaxation, and the influence of baryonic processes.
\end{minipage}%

\begin{minipage}{0.7\textwidth}
\subsubsection{Self-Interacting and Fuzzy Dark Matter} \label{subsubsec:sidm_fdm}

The central cusp predicted by collisionless CDM is, in principle, a sharp and testable prediction; several proposed extensions to the standard cold, collisionless dark matter paradigm instead predict a flattened central \bfit{core}, motivated in part by observations of low-surface-brightness and dwarf galaxies that appear to favour cored profiles over cusps.

\medskip

In \textbf{self-interacting dark matter} (SIDM) models, dark matter particles scatter off each other, allowing energy to flow from the warmer outer regions inward, thereby reducing central densities and transforming steep cusps into flatter cores. Even relatively modest cross-sections (such as $\sigma/m \sim 1\,\text{cm}^2/\text{g}$) can produce kiloparsec-scale cores with central densities as low as $10^7$-$10^8\,M_\odot/\text{kpc}^3$, significantly lower than those predicted by standard CDM. If SIDM effects operate universally, one would expect to find cored profiles at all halo masses, from dwarf galaxies to clusters. However, baryonic processes like gas cooling and stellar feedback can also alter halo density profiles, so observational constraints on SIDM are inevitably complicated by astrophysical uncertainties and depend strongly on modeling choices.
\end{minipage}%

\subsection{Merging Clusters: the Bullet Cluster and El Gordo} \label{subsec:merging_clusters_dm}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
Cluster mergers offer a clean laboratory for testing the collisionless nature of dark matter, because a merger physically separates the cluster's three principal mass components according to their different collisional properties. The hot ICM (traced in X-rays) is baryonic and strongly self-interacting via ram pressure and shocks, and is consequently decelerated and displaced during the collision; the galaxies, in contrast, are effectively collisionless and pass through the merger largely unimpeded; and the dark matter (mapped via lensing) similarly passes through with minimal interaction, tracking the galaxies rather than the shocked gas.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.41\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/bullet.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Bullet Cluster composite: X-ray (baryonic gas) vs.\ weak-lensing mass reconstruction (dark matter)}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

The \bfit{Bullet Cluster} is a striking example where the spatial offset between X-ray (baryonic) emission and weak-lensing (total mass) peaks, together with the observed bow shock, provides some of the clearest evidence for a dominant, collisionless dark matter component, and places an upper limit on its self-interaction cross-section. \bfit{El Gordo} (ACT-CL J0102-4915, $z \approx 0.87$, $M \approx 3\times10^{15}\,M_\odot$), a major merger of two massive clusters at high redshift, shows similar offsets among its hot gas, galaxies, and dark matter, extending these tests to more massive and earlier systems. 

\smallskip

Comparing the observed offsets in El Gordo to simulations suggests a moderate self-interaction cross-section, $\sigma_{\text{DM}}/m_X \sim 4$-$5\,\text{cm}^2/\text{g}$; however, this value tends to predict cored subcluster halos, inconsistent with the cuspy profiles indicated by lensing. This tension between offset-based and profile-based constraints highlights the challenge of tightly constraining dark matter properties from individual merging systems.

\subsection{Tests of General Relativity} \label{subsec:gr_tests_clusters}

\subsubsection{Anisotropic Stress from Lensing and Dynamics} \label{subsubsec:anisotropic_stress}

In the perturbed FLRW metric,
$$
ds^2 = -\left(1+\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right)c^2\,dt^2 + a^2(t)\left(1-\frac{2\Psi}{c^2}\right)\left[dr^2+r^2\,d\Omega^2\right] ,
$$
the potential $\Phi$ perturbs the time-time component of the metric and governs the motion of slow, non-relativistic tracers (such as cluster member galaxies), while $\Psi$ perturbs the spatial part of the metric and, together with $\Phi$, governs the deflection of light. 

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}

    In standard General Relativity these two potentials are equal, $\Phi = \Psi$, in the absence of anisotropic stress; many modified-gravity theories, by contrast, predict $\Phi \neq \Psi$. Because gravitational lensing (\cref{subsubsec:lensing_mass}) is sensitive to the sum $\Phi+\Psi$, while galaxy dynamics (\cref{subsubsec:dynamical_mass}) is sensitive only to $\Psi$, comparing the lensing-derived and dynamically derived mass profiles of the same cluster directly constrains the \textbf{anisotropic stress parameter}, $\eta \equiv \Psi/\Phi$ (equal to unity in GR), which is given by:
    $$
    \eta(r) = \frac{\int_r^\infty \left[2M_{\text{lens}}(r') - M_{\text{dyn}}(r')\right]\frac{dr'}{r'^2}}{\int_r^\infty M_{\text{dyn}}(r')\,\frac{dr'}{r'^2}} .
    $$
    Applied to a sample of nine massive clusters from the CLASH-VLT survey, this comparison yields a combined constraint on $\eta$ fully consistent with the GR expectation $\eta=1$, providing an independent, cluster-scale test of gravity that complements Solar System and binary-pulsar tests at very different mass and length scales.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/clash-vlt.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Constraints on the anisotropic stress parameter $\eta$ from 9 CLASH-VLT clusters, showing individual and combined posteriors.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip



\subsubsection{Gravitational Redshift in Cluster Potentials} \label{subsubsec:gravitational_redshift}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
Photons escaping a gravitational potential well are redshifted by an amount $z_{\text{gr}} = \nabla\Phi/c^2$; for a cluster of mass $\sim10^{14}\,M_\odot$, this corresponds to $c\,z_{\text{gr}} \sim 10\,\text{km/s}$, roughly two orders of magnitude smaller than the Doppler broadening from the cluster's internal velocity dispersion ($\sim1000\,\text{km/s}$), and therefore undetectable in any individual galaxy's redshift. Because galaxies near the cluster centre sit deeper in the potential well than those at large radius, however, the gravitational redshift shift $\Delta(R)$ is predicted to be most negative near the centre and to rise (become less negative) toward the outskirts; critically, gravitational redshift shifts the \textit{centroid} of the velocity distribution, whereas ordinary Doppler broadening only symmetrically widens it, leaving the centroid unaffected.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/gr.png}
    \captionof{figure}{stacked line-of-sight velocity distributions in radial bins from 7800 SDSS clusters, showing the systematic centroid shift}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

This asymmetry can therefore be isolated statistically by stacking the velocity distributions of member galaxies, in radial bins, across a very large cluster sample, using the brightest cluster galaxy as the reference rest frame; applying this technique to some 7\,800 clusters from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey yields a clear detection of the predicted centroid shift, in agreement with the GR prediction and disfavouring the larger deviations predicted by some modified-gravity alternatives (such as $f(R)$ gravity or TeVeS) at the level probed by the current sample.

\section{Large-Scale Structure and Clustering} \label{sec:clustering}

Beyond the abundance of individual collapsed halos (\cref{sec:cluster_number_counts}), the spatial arrangement of matter itself, how it clumps, connects, and correlates across cosmic volumes, encodes a further wealth of cosmological information. This section develops the statistical tools used to characterise this \bfit{large-scale structure} (LSS), traces its origin back to the seed perturbations generated during inflation, and shows how its observed clustering pattern is used to constrain both the expansion history and the growth of structure.

\subsection{The Growth of Density Perturbations} \label{subsec:growth_perturbations}

Inflation (\cref{subsubsec:inflation}) generates the primordial density field by amplifying microscopic quantum fluctuations and stretching them to astrophysical scales. A fluctuation of comoving wavenumber $k$ begins deep inside the Hubble radius, oscillating like an ordinary quantum field; as the exponential expansion proceeds, its physical wavelength grows faster than the horizon, and the mode eventually crosses outside it ($k = aH$). Beyond this point of \textbf{horizon exit}, causal microphysics can no longer act on the fluctuation, and it \textit{freezes} as a super-horizon curvature perturbation, $\delta\phi_k \to \zeta_k$. Only once the horizon grows large enough, after inflation ends and the Universe reheats, does the mode \textit{re-enter} the horizon, at which point gravity can once again act on it and drive the \textbf{growth of the density perturbation} $\delta_m(\mathbf{x})$ that ultimately seeds galaxies, clusters, and the cosmic web.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/density-perturbations.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Inflation stretches miscroscopic quantum fluctations to super-horizon scales, where they freeze as curvature perturbations and later seed the growth of cosmic structures.}
\end{center}
\medskip
$$
\left[
\delta\phi_k \rightarrow \zeta_k \rightarrow \delta_m(\mathbf{x})
\right]
$$

\medskip

The simplest single-field inflationary models predict that these initial fluctuations form a \textbf{Gaussian random field}: statistically homogeneous and isotropic, nearly scale-invariant, and adiabatic. The primordial power spectrum is then fully specified by an amplitude $A_s$ and a spectral tilt $n_s$ about a pivot scale $k_p = 0.05\,\text{Mpc}^{-1}$:
$$
P(k) = A_s \left(\frac{k}{k_p}\right)^{n_s}
$$
A perfectly scale-invariant spectrum corresponds to $n_s = 1$ (the Harrison-Zel'dovich spectrum); Planck measures $n_s \simeq 0.965$, a small but highly significant deviation that is itself a key prediction of inflation, since the slow but nonzero rolling of the inflaton field breaks exact scale invariance.

\medskip

Once fluctuations re-enter the horizon, their subsequent evolution depends on the era (radiation- or matter-dominated) and on whether the perturbation is still super- or already sub-horizon. Defining the \textbf{overdensity field}:
$$
\delta(\mathbf{x}) \equiv \frac{\rho(\mathbf{x}) - \langle\rho\rangle}{\langle\rho\rangle} ,
$$
the growth of cold dark matter (CDM) and radiation perturbations in the two regimes is:

\medskip

\begin{center}
\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.5}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
    & \makecell{$z > z_{\text{eq}}$\\radiation-dominated} 
    & \makecell{$z < z_{\text{eq}}$\\matter-dominated} \\
\midrule
\makecell{$R > R_H$\\(super-horizon)} 
    & $\delta_r \propto \delta_m \propto a^2$ 
    & $\delta_r \propto \delta_m \propto a$ \\
\makecell{$R < R_H$\\(sub-horizon)} 
    & \makecell{$\delta_{\text{cdm}} \sim \text{const}$\\$\delta_{b+\gamma}$ oscillates}
    & \makecell{$\delta_{\text{cdm}} \propto a$\\$z<z_\text{rec}\!: \, \delta_b \to \delta_{\text{cdm}}$} \\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

The key feature of this table is the freezing of sub-horizon CDM growth during radiation domination, known as the \bfit{Mészáros effect}: because radiation dominates the total energy density and expansion rate at that epoch, the gravitational forcing on the (subdominant) dark matter is too weak to overcome the rapid Hubble expansion, so $\delta_{\text{cdm}}$ stagnates until \textbf{matter-radiation equality}:

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
    $$
    \frac{\rho_m(a_{\text{eq}})}{\rho_r(a_{\text{eq}})} = 1
    \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad
    1 + z_{\text{eq}} = \frac{a_0}{a_{\text{eq}}} \simeq 3250 ,
    $$

    after which CDM perturbations grow freely as $\delta_{\text{cdm}} \propto a$.
    
    \smallskip
    
    Baryons, by contrast, remain tightly coupled to photons via Compton scattering until recombination, and so continue to oscillate as a photon-baryon fluid (\cref{sec:cmb_anisotropies}) even after equality; only once this coupling breaks do baryons fall into the potential wells already carved out by the dark matter, $\delta_b \to \delta_{\text{cdm}}$. The Hubble radius entering this classification is simply $R_H(t) = c/H(t) \sim ct$.

    \subsubsection{The Transfer Function} \label{subsubsec:transfer_function}

    The net result of this scale-dependent evolution is that the primordial power spectrum is not preserved unaltered down to late times, but is reprocessed by gravitational growth into the power spectrum actually observed today,
    $$
    P(k,z) = P_{\text{primordial}}(k)\, T^2(k,z) ,
    $$
    where the \textbf{transfer function} $T(k,z)$ encodes the differential growth history of each Fourier mode. Modes that were always super-horizon prior to $z_{\text{eq}}$ (small $k$) grow unimpeded, while modes that entered the horizon during radiation domination (large $k$) suffer the Mészáros suppression described above; the transition scale is set by the horizon size at equality, $k_{\text{eq}} = 2\pi (c\,t_{\text{eq}})^{-1} \propto \Omega_{m,0} h^2$.

    \smallskip

    For CDM, a simple approximation for $T(k)$ is:
    $$
    T(k) \simeq
    \begin{cases}
    1 , & k \lesssim k_{\text{eq}} \\
    (k/k_{\text{eq}})^{-2} , & k \gtrsim k_{\text{eq}}
    \end{cases}
    $$
\end{minipage}%
\hfill

\smallskip

    \vspace{-0.5em}

\medskip

Consequently, if the primordial spectrum follows $P_{\text{primordial}}(k) \propto k^{n_s}$, the processed spectrum steepens across the horizon scale at equality:
\medskip
$$
P(k) \propto k^{n_s} \quad (k \ll k_{\text{eq}}, \text{ above horizon}), \qquad
P(k) \propto k^{n_s - 4} \quad (k \gg k_{\text{eq}}, \text{ below horizon}) ,
$$
producing the characteristic broad turnover visible in the matter power spectrum at $k \sim k_{\text{eq}}$.

\subsubsection{Hot, warm, and cold dark matter}

Dark matter candidates are classified by their kinematic state at decoupling. \bfit{Thermal relics} remain in equilibrium with the primordial plasma until their interaction rate $\Gamma = n\langle\sigma v\rangle$ falls below the expansion rate $H(a)$; if the particle is still relativistic at that moment ($3k_B T_d > m_X c^2$), it constitutes \textbf{hot dark matter} (e.g.\ light, still-relativistic neutrinos), whereas a non-relativistic decoupling ($3k_B T_d < m_X c^2$) yields \textbf{cold dark matter} (e.g.\ WIMPs). An intermediate case, \textbf{warm dark matter} (e.g.\ keV sterile neutrino), decouples while mildly relativistic. \bfit{Non-thermal relics}, such as axions or cosmic strings, are never in thermal equilibrium with the rest of the Universe in the first place.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.54\textwidth}
    The observational signature of a non-cold dark matter component is \bfit{free-streaming damping}: particles with non-negligible thermal velocity travel a characteristic free-streaming length $\lambda_{\text{fs}}$ before becoming non-relativistic, erasing density perturbations on scales below the corresponding wavenumber:
    \medskip
    $$
    k_{\text{FS}} \sim 15.6\,\frac{h}{\text{Mpc}}\left(\frac{m_{\text{WDM}}}{1\,\text{keV}}\right)^{4/3}\left(\frac{0.12}{\Omega_{\text{DM}}h^2}\right)^{1/3}
    $$

    This same mechanism causes the neutrino suppression of the halo mass function (\cref{subsubsec:hmf_neutrinos}); measuring the small-scale power spectrum cutoff (e.g.\ via the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest) sets some of the strongest limits on warm dark matter masses.
\end{minipage}%

\subsubsection{Baryon Acoustic Oscillations: Physical Origin} \label{subsubsec:bao_origin}

Prior to recombination, baryons and photons are tightly coupled by Compton scattering into a single photon-baryon fluid. Within this fluid, gravity acts to compress overdense regions while radiation pressure resists compression, setting up standing acoustic waves, these are the same oscillations that give rise to the acoustic peaks in the CMB power spectrum (\cref{subsubsec:cmb_powerspectrum}). As the Universe cools and recombination occurs, the interaction rate between baryons and photons drops sharply: photons decouple and begin to free-stream, while baryons are left behind, distributed in a thin, roughly spherical shell around each initial overdensity. 

\medskip

The radius of this shell is set by the comoving distance sound waves can travel before decoupling, known as the \bfit{sound horizon}. Thereafter, only gravity couples the now decoupled baryons to the dark matter. This imprints a characteristic excess in the late-time matter distribution at a fixed physical scale, corresponding to the \textbf{baryon acoustic oscillations} (BAO). In the matter power spectrum, this appears as a series of oscillatory wiggles at the characteristic wavenumber $k_{\text{BAO}}$, which are the small-amplitude “twinkles” visible in \cref{fig:tf} just after the orange vertical line, further discussed in \cref{subsec:bao}.

\subsection{The Two-Point Correlation Function} \label{subsec:2pcf}

The most direct statistical description of clustering is the \bfit{two-point correlation function} (2PCF), $\xi(r)$: the fractional excess probability, relative to a spatially unclustered (Poisson) distribution, of finding two tracers of the density field (galaxies, clusters, or the underlying dark matter itself) separated by a distance $r$:
\medskip
$$
dP = n\,[1 + \xi(r)]\,dV ,
$$
where $n$ is the mean number density of the tracer population. A vanishing correlation function, $\xi(r) = 0$, recovers the purely Poissonian expectation, $dP = n\,dV$.

\medskip


\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
In practice, $\xi(r)$ is measured by comparing the observed pair counts in a survey, $DD(r)$, against pair counts computed from a random catalogue with the same geometry and selection function, $RR(r)$, and the cross-pairs between data and randoms, $DR(r)$. Two widely used estimators are the original \textbf{Peebles-Hauser} estimator and the \textbf{Landy-Szalay} estimator, the latter offering reduced sensitivity to survey-boundary effects and shot noise:
$$
\hat\xi_{\text{PH}}(r) = \frac{DD(r) - RR(r)}{RR(r)}
$$
$$
\hat\xi_{\text{LS}}(r) = \frac{DD(r) - 2\,DR(r) + RR(r)}{RR(r)} .
$$
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/2pt.png}
\captionof{figure}{Examples of the two-point correlation function for a random (left) and clustered (right) distribution.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Assuming the Universe is statistically isotropic, the correlation function depends only on the scalar separation $r = |\vec r|$, and can be written as an ensemble average of the density field itself:
\medskip
$$
\xi(\vec r) = \big\langle \delta(\vec x)\,\delta(\vec x + \vec r) \big\rangle .
$$
The 2PCF and the matter power spectrum $P(k)$ form a Fourier transform pair:
\medskip
$$
\xi(\vec r) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int d^3k\; P(\vec k)\, e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r} , \qquad\qquad
P(\vec k) = \int d^3r\; \xi(\vec r)\, e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r} ,
$$
so that, for a Gaussian random field, where the overdensity field is defined to have zero mean, either statistic alone completely characterises the density field; all information is contained in the second moment, and higher-order correlations vanish.

\begin{observationblock}[Non-linear evolution and the loss of Gaussianity]
In the linear regime ($\delta \ll 1$), each Fourier mode of the density field evolves independently, and the power spectrum simply rescales in amplitude via the linear growth factor $D(z)$,
\medskip
$$
P_{\text{lin}}(k,z) = D(z)^2\, P_{\text{lin}}(k, z=0) .
$$
Once perturbations become non-linear ($\delta \gtrsim 1$), however, different Fourier modes begin to couple to one another, and no closed analytic solution exists for the subsequent evolution; N-body simulations are required to follow the density field into this regime. A direct consequence of this mode-coupling is that the density field loses its Gaussian character: the two-point function (or power spectrum) alone no longer fully specifies the field, and higher-order statistics (the bispectrum, trispectrum, and beyond) carry genuinely new information at small scales.
\end{observationblock}

\subsection{Biased Tracers of the Density Field} \label{subsec:bias}

Neither galaxies nor galaxy clusters directly sample the underlying matter density field; both are \bfit{biased tracers}, in the same sense that the distribution of city lights on Earth traces, but does not linearly reproduce, the distribution of landmass and population density. Halo formation is not a Poisson sampling of the matter field: as established by the spherical collapse picture of \cref{subsec:spherical_collapse}, halos form only where the smoothed density field locally exceeds the critical collapse threshold $\delta_c$. Because the small-scale density field is itself modulated by long-wavelength (large-scale) modes, regions that are already overdense on large scales contain an enhanced abundance of collapsed halos, so that halos display systematically stronger clustering than the underlying matter distribution from which they formed.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
To leading order, this effect is captured by a linear \bfit{bias} parameter $b_h$, relating the halo and matter overdensity fields:
\vspace{-0.5em}
$$
\delta_h = b_h\, \delta_m
$$
The halo correlation function and power spectrum then follow directly as:
\medskip
$$
\xi_{hh}(r) = \langle\,\delta_h(x)\,\delta_h(x+r)\,\rangle = b_h^2\, \xi_{mm}(r)
$$

\vspace{-1.5em}

$$
\mathcal{F} \Big\uparrow \Big\downarrow \mathcal{F}^{-1} 
$$
$$
P_{hh}(k) = \langle\,\tilde{\delta}_h(k)\,\tilde{\delta}_h^*(k')\,\rangle = b_h^2\, P_{mm}(k)
$$
Because more massive halos form at rarer, higher peaks of the density field, the bias grows monotonically with halo mass (and, at fixed mass, with redshift, since a given mass threshold corresponds to a rarer peak at earlier times, when the overall variance of the field was smaller). 

\medskip

Galaxy clusters, forming at the very highest peaks of the density field, therefore carry the largest bias of any commonly used tracer, and this bias can be predicted directly from the halo mass function of \cref{subsec:halo_mass_function}. This large bias comes at a cost, however. Because clusters form at the rarest density peaks, they are also an intrinsically \bfit{sparse} tracer of $\delta_m$: their number density is orders of magnitude lower than that of galaxies ($\sim 10^{3\text{-}4}$ objects in a typical survey, compared to $\sim 10^{8}$ galaxies), so their clustering signal is significantly degraded by shot noise.

\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.38\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/halo-bias.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Halo bias $b_h$ versus mass, curves for several redshifts, increasing with both mass and redshift}
\medskip
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/DESY6.png}
\captionof{figure}{Matter power spectrum $P_m(k)$ from different surveys.}
\label{fig:DESY6}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Galaxies, in exchange for a much higher number density and correspondingly lower shot noise, have a bias that cannot be predicted from first-principles halo-collapse theory alone, and instead depends sensitively on the specific galaxy sample used (its colour and magnitude selection, in particular), requiring empirical calibration against simulations or joint fits to the data themselves.

\medskip

\cref{fig:DESY6} shows $P_m(k)$ measured by various surveys. DES Y6 galaxies (orange, $z<1$) probe the low-redshift Universe, where non-linear evolution affects small scales, allowing us to study the growth of structure beyond the linear regime. In contrast, high-redshift tracers like the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest (grey, $z>2$) measure $P_m(k)$ before significant non-linearities, and CMB data (Planck, colored points) reveal the primordial, linear spectrum. This multi-probe approach links the initial conditions to present-day structure.

\subsection{Spectroscopic versus Photometric Redshift Surveys} \label{subsec:surveys_specphot}

The clustering signal is intrinsically three-dimensional, but while the angular position of a tracer on the sky is generally straightforward to measure, its radial (line-of-sight) distance can only be inferred from a redshift measurement, whose precision depends critically on the observing technique.

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Spectroscopic redshifts} ($\sigma_z \sim 0.001\,(1+z)$) are obtained by directly measuring a galaxy's spectrum and identifying the Doppler shift of known emission or absorption features. Because the light must be dispersed into narrow wavelength bins, only a small fraction of the total flux falls in each bin, requiring long integration times and effectively restricting the technique to sufficiently luminous sources.
\item \textbf{Photometric redshifts} ($\sigma_z \sim 0.01\,(1+z)$) are instead estimated from broadband imaging, by fitting template spectral energy distributions (or comparing against galaxies with known spectroscopic redshift). Because photometric bins are far wider, comparable signal-to-noise is reached with much shorter exposures, and wide-field imaging cameras cover far greater sky areas than multi-object spectrographs.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

These complementary strengths translate directly into the character of the resulting survey.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
\bfit{Spectroscopic surveys} deliver accurate three-dimensional reconstructions of the cosmic web, but at the cost of shallower depth, lower source density, and stronger selection effects, since only the intrinsically brightest sources can be targeted efficiently. \bfit{Photometric surveys}, in contrast, sacrifice line-of-sight resolution for depth, area, and source density, typically probing much larger cosmic volumes with comparatively mild selection effects.

\medskip

Because a spectroscopic redshift error directly perturbs the inferred radial distance of a tracer, it systematically degrades the clustering measurement along the line of sight: the slope of the 2PCF is artificially reduced, and (most notably) the sharp BAO feature discussed below is smeared out and partially washed away, since pairs that are truly close together in comoving distance are scattered to a broader range of apparent separations.

\medskip

Moreover, spectroscopic surveys, thanks to their accurately measured redshifts, yield a sharp three-dimensional correlation function in which the baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) bump around $100$~Mpc/$h$ is clearly visible. In contrast, photometric surveys suffer from substantial projection effects because redshift uncertainties blur the true radial distances. This smearing smooths out features in the correlation function, making the BAO bump undetectable and reducing the overall precision of large-scale clustering measurements. As a result, while photometric surveys have advantages in area and depth, their scientific reach for certain cosmological measurements (such as precise standard-ruler constraints from the BAO) remains limited by projection effects.
\end{minipage}%

\subsubsection{The Angular Two-Point Correlation Function} \label{subsubsec:angular_2pcf}

When the radial resolution of a survey is poor (as in a photometric survey) or entirely absent (as for the last-scattering surface of the CMB), the three-dimensional correlation function cannot be reconstructed directly, and one instead measures the \bfit{angular correlation function} $w(\theta)$, defined in direct analogy with $\xi(r)$ as the excess probability of finding two tracers separated by an angular distance $\theta$ on the sky:
$$
dP(\theta) = n\,[1+w(\theta)]\, d\Omega
$$
This angular statistic is related to the full three-dimensional correlation function via a line-of-sight projection over the redshift distributions $r(z_1)$, $r(z_2)$ of the two populations being correlated:
\medskip
$$
w(\theta) = \int\!\!\int dz_1\,dz_2\; \xi\!\left(\sqrt{r^2(z_1) + r^2(z_2) - 2\,r(z_1)\,r(z_2)\cos\theta}\right)
$$

\vspace{-0.5em}

\begin{minipage}{0.72\textwidth}
Equivalently, $w(\theta)$ can be expanded in Legendre polynomials $L_\ell(\cos\theta)$ and related to the \bfit{angular power spectrum} $C_\ell$:
\medskip
$$
w(\theta) = \sum_\ell \frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\, L_\ell(\cos\theta)\, C_\ell, 
$$
$$
C_\ell = 2\pi n_\Omega \int_{-1}^{+1} w(\theta)\, L_\ell(\cos\theta)\, d\cos\theta
$$
This is the same formalism used for the CMB temperature and polarisation power spectra in \cref{subsubsec:cmb_powerspectrum}, applied to the projected galaxy density field of photometric surveys.
\end{minipage}%

\subsection{Baryon Acoustic Oscillations as a Standard Ruler} \label{subsec:bao}

The physical mechanism generating the BAO was introduced in \cref{subsubsec:bao_origin}; here we develop its use as a cosmological probe. The key point is that the propagation of the acoustic waves in the early Universe (determined by the sound speed of the photon-baryon fluid and the timing of the drag epoch) is governed by well-understood microphysics. Consequently, the (comoving) sound horizon at the drag epoch, $r_d$, can be predicted robustly from first principles and is largely insensitive to late-time cosmology. The \textbf{sound horizon} at drag epoch is defined as the distance that sound can travel in the baryon-photon plasma before baryons decouple from photons at $z_d \approx 1060 \approx z_*$:
\medskip
$$
r_d = \int_{z_d}^{\infty} \frac{c_s(z)}{H(z)}\,dz , \qquad
c_s(z) = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3\left(1 + \dfrac{3\rho_B(z)}{4\rho_\gamma(z)}\right)}} ,
$$
where $c_s(z)$ is the sound speed in the coupled photon-baryon fluid, depending on the baryon ($\rho_b$) and photon ($\rho_\gamma$) energy densities. A widely used fitting formula for the value of $r_d$ is
\medskip
$$
r_d \simeq 147.05\,\mathrm{Mpc} \left(\frac{\omega_m}{0.1432}\right)^{-0.23} \left(\frac{N_{\text{eff}}}{3.04}\right)^{-0.1} \left(\frac{\omega_b}{0.02236}\right)^{-0.13}
$$
where $\omega_m \equiv \Omega_m h^2$ and $\omega_b \equiv \Omega_b h^2$ are the present-day (physical) matter and baryon densities, and $N_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective number of relativistic species. An increase in $N_{\text{eff}}$ leads to a faster expansion rate, reducing $r_d$.

\medskip

Because its physical length is fixed by early-Universe physics alone, $r_d$ functions as a \bfit{standard ruler}: comparing this known comoving length to its \textit{observed} angular or redshift extent at any given epoch directly constrains the late-time expansion history.

Thus, by comparing the known comoving size of the sound horizon to its measured angular or redshift extent at different epochs, we can directly track the evolution of cosmic distances and probe the geometry of the Universe.

\subsubsection{Angular Diameter Distance}

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
    This comparison can be performed in two independent directions. \bfit{Perpendicular} to the line of sight, the ruler subtends a preferred angular separation
\medskip
$$
\theta = \frac{r_d(z)}{(1+z)\,d_A(z)}
$$
directly probing the angular diameter distance $d_A(z)$. \bfit{Parallel} to the line of sight, the same physical length instead maps onto a preferred redshift separation:
\medskip
$$
\Delta z = \frac{H(z)\, r_d}{c}
$$
directly probing the Hubble parameter $H(z)$ itself. Measuring both directions simultaneously therefore yields two independent geometric constraints from a single, well-understood standard ruler.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.38\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{assets/bao-ruler.png}
    \captionof{figure}{BAO standard ruler: comoving sphere of radius $r_d$ subtending angle $\theta$ (perpendicular) and redshift interval $\Delta z$ (parallel) to the observer.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

In a spatially flat $\Lambda$CDM Universe at redshifts well below matter-radiation equality ($z \ll z_{\text{eq}}$), the \textbf{angular diameter distance} and \textbf{Hubble parameter} are given by:ù
\medskip
$$
d_A(z) = \frac{c}{H_0}\,\frac{1}{1+z}\int_0^z \frac{dz'}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z')^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}} , \qquad
H(z) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\Lambda,0}}
$$
These are the same expressions introduced previously in \cref{subsec:luminosity_distance}.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
The angular diameter distance $d_A(z)$ rises rapidly with redshift at first, reaches a maximum (the "cosmic lens" or "focus" effect), and then decreases at higher $z$ as objects appear smaller. The exact location and height of this maximum depend on the matter and dark energy density: higher $\Omega_{m,0}$ or positive curvature shift the peak to lower $z$ and lower $d_A$, while a cosmological constant (flat, $\Omega_{\Lambda,0} > 0$) increases $d_A$ at high $z$. 

\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/Angular-diam-dist.png}
    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left:} Angular diameter distance $d_A(z)$ versus redshift. \textbf{Right:} Angular scale (arcmin/Mpc) of a fixed physical ruler as a function of redshift}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Intuitively, we expect that a more distant object should always appear smaller on the sky—a behaviour that would indeed occur in an "empty universe" ($\Omega_{m,0} = 0$, $\Omega_{k,0} = 1$). However, in realistic cosmologies with matter and dark energy, the angular scale subtended by a standard ruler actually decreases with redshift only up to the maximum of $d_A(z)$, and then starts to increase again for more distant objects. This non-monotonic behaviour means that sufficiently distant galaxies can actually appear larger in angular size than nearer ones. The detailed redshift at which this "cosmic lens" effect occurs, as well as the minimum angular scale, depend sensitively on the values of $\Omega_{m,0}$ and $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}$.

\medskip

The turnover point occurs because of the expansion of the universe and the finite speed of light: very distant objects were closer to us when they emitted the light we see today. At that time they spanned a larger angle.

\begin{minipage}{0.65\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/apparent-angle.png}
    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left:} At high redshift, objects can appear larger because their light was emitted when they were closer to us.}

    \medskip

    Very distant objects do not always appear smaller as they recede. Beyond a critical redshift, the expansion of the universe and the finite speed of light mean we receive light that was emitted when the object was much closer to us than its current distance. Thus, extremely distant galaxies can actually span a larger apparent angle, even though they are farther away now. This counterintuitive effect, linked to the evolution of the angular diameter distance with redshift, is a clear signature of the universe's geometry and expansion history.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.32\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/apparent-angle-1.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Galaxy $Y$ is now farther than $X$, but was physically closer when it emitted the light we see today.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.52\textwidth}
    In observations, the BAO feature shows up as a single broad bump in the galaxy two-point correlation function, $\xi(r)$, at $r \sim 100$-$130\,h^{-1}\,\text{Mpc}$, on top of a smooth background. In Fourier space, this becomes a set of damped oscillatory "wiggles" in the galaxy power spectrum $P(k)$. Because the amplitude is small ($\Omega_b \ll \Omega_m$), early surveys like BOSS needed samples of over a million galaxies to detect the signal clearly above noise.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/baryonic-peak.png}
    \captionof{figure}{BAO peak in galaxy 2-pt correlation function (real and Fourier space).}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Beyond galaxies, the same physical scale imprints an 
analogous signature in quasar clustering and in the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest (\cref
{subsec:lyman_alpha}), whose absorption pattern traces the distribution of neutral 
hydrogen along many independent lines of sight and so offers a probe of the BAO scale 
at higher redshift ($z \sim 2$-$3$) than galaxy samples alone can reach.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
Once imprinted at the drag epoch, the BAO scale preserves its comoving size $r_d$ and simply expands with the Hubble flow, acting as a cosmological standard ruler. By measuring the angular scale $\alpha_\perp$ (often from the observed BAO angle $\theta_{\text{BAO}}(z)$) and the line-of-sight scale $\alpha_\parallel$ (from the BAO redshift separation $\Delta z_{\text{BAO}}(z)$) across different redshifts, modern spectroscopic surveys map out both the angular diameter distance $d_A(z)$ and the Hubble parameter $H(z)$ as a function of cosmic time. This provides a precise geometric probe of the Universe's expansion history, dark energy, and spatial curvature. When combined with an external calibration of the sound horizon $r_d$ (e.g., from CMB measurements or Big Bang nucleosynthesis), BAO serve as an independent route to determining the Hubble constant $H_0$.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.39\textwidth}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/comoving.png}
    \captionof{figure}{How transverse ($\alpha_\perp$) and LOS ($\alpha_\parallel$) measurements of the BAO at different redshifts constrain the expansion history via $d_A(z)$ and $H(z)$.}
\end{minipage}

\subsection{Redshift-Space Distortions} \label{subsec:rsd}

A subtlety complicates the direct translation of an observed redshift into a true, physical distance: the observed redshift of a galaxy is not purely cosmological, but also carries the imprint of its \textbf{peculiar velocity}, $v_{\text{pec}}$, the galaxy's motion relative to the smooth Hubble flow, itself sourced gravitationally by the surrounding density field,
$$
v = v_{\text{exp}} + v_{\text{pec}} = H_0\,d(z) + v_{\text{pec}} , \qquad
z_{\text{obs}} = z_{\text{exp}} + \frac{v_{\text{pec}}}{c}\,(1+z_{\text{exp}})
$$
The proper distance inferred naively from the observed redshift, the \bfit{redshift-space} distance, therefore differs systematically from the true, real-space distance:
$$
s = \frac{v}{H_0} = d(z) + \frac{v_{\text{pec}}}{H_0} .
$$
Because only the LOS component of the peculiar velocity affects the observed redshift (transverse motions leave the angular position unaffected), this introduces a purely radial distortion: the two-point correlation function, isotropic in real space, becomes anisotropic once measured in redshift space. Decomposing the pair separation into components perpendicular ($\sigma$) and parallel ($\pi$) to the LOS allows this anisotropy to be characterised via $\xi(\sigma,\pi)$.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.62\textwidth}
Two distinct physical regimes produce opposite distortion patterns. On large scales, peculiar velocities trace the coherent, linear infall of matter toward overdensities: material on the near side of a cluster moves away from the observer (partially cancelling its Hubble recession) while material on the far side moves toward the observer (compounding it), so that a spherical overdensity appears compressed, or "squashed", along the line of sight in redshift space. This is the \textbf{Kaiser effect}. On small scales, by contrast, the virialised, essentially random orbital velocities of galaxies within a single collapsed halo dominate, and these highly nonlinear motions instead stretch a compact structure into an elongated radial feature, the well-known \textbf{Finger-of-God effect}, so named for its characteristic appearance in redshift-space maps of individual clusters.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.35\textwidth}
    \vspace{-0.5em}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/real-vs-z.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Kaiser squashing versus Finger-of-God stretching.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Peculiar velocities from gravitational clustering affect only the radial (LOS) distances, causing systematic distortions in \bfit{redshift space}: the correlation function, isotropic in real space, appears \textbf{anisotropic} in redshift space.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}

To quantify this, we decompose the separation between two galaxies into components parallel and perpendicular to the line of sight:
\medskip
$$
\pi \equiv \frac{|\vec{S} \cdot \vec{l}|}{|\vec{l}|}, 
\qquad
\sigma \equiv \sqrt{|\vec{S}|^2 - \pi^2}
$$
where $\vec{S} = \vec{s}_1 - \vec{s}_2$ is the separation vector, and $\vec{l} = (\vec{s}_1 + \vec{s}_2)/2$ is their mean position. We then measure the correlation function as $\xi(\sigma, \pi)$, which directly reveals the anisotropic structure: "squashing" on large scales (Kaiser effect) and "stretching" on small scales (Finger-of-God).

\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}

    \vspace{-1em}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/kaiser-fog.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{\textbf{Left:} Geometry of $\sigma$ and $\pi$ for a galaxy pair. \textbf{Right:} 2dFGRS $\xi(\sigma, \pi)$: LOS stretch (Finger-of-God) at small scales; LOS compression (Kaiser effect) at large scales.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

In the linear regime, the Kaiser effect can be derived analytically, yielding a simple, anisotropic rescaling of the density and power-spectrum amplitudes in Fourier space,
$$
\delta_s(\vec k) = \left(1 + \beta\mu^2\right)\delta_r(\vec k) , \qquad
P_s(k) = \left(1 + \beta\mu^2\right)^2 P_r(\vec k) ,
$$
where $\mu$ is the cosine of the angle between the wavevector and the line of sight, and $\beta$ combines the linear growth rate $f(\Omega_m)$ with the tracer's bias $b$ (\cref{subsec:bias}),
$$
\beta = \frac{f(\Omega_m)}{b} .
$$
\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
Because $f(\Omega_m) \equiv d\ln\delta/d\ln a$ measures precisely how efficiently gravity is currently amplifying density perturbations, a measurement of the RSD-induced anisotropy provides a direct, model-independent constraint on the combination $f\sigma_8(z)$, the amplitude of structure growth as a function of cosmic time. This makes RSD a genuinely complementary probe to the geometric information carried by BAO: whereas BAO tests the Universe's expansion history, RSD tests the growth of structure within that expansion history, and any departure between the two (for instance, a growth rate lower than $\Lambda$CDM predicts at fixed expansion history) would be a direct signature of modified gravity, echoing the anisotropic-stress tests of \cref{subsubsec:anisotropic_stress}.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/z-dist-probe.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Measurements of $f\sigma_8$ from redshift-space distortions vs.\ redshift, compared to $\Lambda$CDM (black).}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

\subsection{The Kinetic SZ Effect as a Growth Probe} \label{subsec:ksz_growth}

The kinetic Sunyaev-Zel'dovich effect, introduced in \cref{subsec:ksz} as a Doppler-induced CMB temperature fluctuation sourced by the bulk motion of cluster gas, offers a further, independent route to the growth rate $f(a)$, complementary to RSD. In the \bfit{pairwise kSZ} technique, the mean relative (infall) velocity of a large sample of cluster pairs at fixed comoving separation $r$ is estimated by stacking the corresponding kSZ temperature signal,
$$
T_{\text{pkSZ}}(r) \equiv \bar\tau_e\,\frac{v_{12}(r)}{c}\,T_{\text{CMB}} ,
$$
where $\bar\tau_e$ is the mean optical depth of the stacked sample. In linear theory, the mean pairwise infall velocity is directly proportional to the growth rate and to the two-point matter correlation function,
$$
v_{12}(r,a) \approx -\frac{2}{3}\,a\,H(a)\,f(a)\,r\,\frac{b\,\bar\xi(r)}{1 + b^2\xi(r)} ,
$$
\begin{minipage}{0.58\textwidth}
so that a measurement of $T_{\text{pkSZ}}(r)$ across a range of separations directly constrains $f(a)$, entirely independently of the RSD formalism above, and without relying on redshift-space distortions of the galaxy field itself. Cross-correlating large photometric or spectroscopic cluster catalogues (such as those from DESI) with high-resolution CMB temperature maps (from Planck, ACT, or SPT) has yielded robust pairwise kSZ detections, providing a genuinely independent cross-check on the growth history inferred from RSD, weak lensing, and cluster abundance (\cref{subsec:cluster_cosmo_constraints}).
\end{minipage}%
\hfill

    \centering

\end{minipage}


\section{The CMB Angular Power Spectrum as a probe} \label{sec:cmb_probe_detail}

\cref{sec:cmb_anisotropies} introduced the CMB angular power spectrum and its acoustic peaks in broad terms; here we develop the physics underlying its shape in more detail, since it is this shape, peak by peak, that makes the CMB one of the tightest cosmological probes available.

\subsection{The Last-Scattering Surface Revisited} \label{subsec:lss_revisited}

Recombination is not an instantaneous process, so the CMB photons we observe do not originate from a single, sharp redshift but from a \bfit{last-scattering surface} (LSS) of finite thickness. It is useful to distinguish two closely related but conceptually distinct redshifts. The \textbf{redshift of recombination}, $z_{\text{rec}} \simeq 1090$, is defined as the epoch at which the free-electron (ionisation) fraction drops below some threshold value, conventionally $x_e \simeq 0.1$. The \textbf{redshift of decoupling}, $z_{\text{dec}} \simeq 1080$, is instead defined dynamically, as the epoch at which the Thomson scattering rate falls below the Hubble expansion rate, $\Gamma_{T} = n_e\,\sigma_T\,c \simeq H(z)$; beyond this point photons can no longer scatter frequently enough to remain coupled to the baryons; and it is $z_{\text{dec}}$, rather than $z_{\text{rec}}$, that effectively coincides with the LSS. Because the transition is gradual, the LSS itself has a finite width, $\Delta z \simeq 80$, which becomes directly relevant to the damping tail of the power spectrum discussed below.

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsection{Decomposing the TT Spectrum} \label{subsec:tt_decomposition}

\subsubsection{Super-Horizon Scales: Sachs-Wolfe effect} \label{subsec:sachs_wolfe}

\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
An important angular scale is set by the comoving Hubble radius at decoupling,
$$
r_H(z_{\text{dec}}) = \frac{c}{H(z_{\text{dec}})}
$$
whose projected angular size on the sky today is:
$$
\theta_H = \frac{r_H}{d_A(z_{\text{dec}})\,(1+z_{\text{dec}})} \simeq 0.87^\circ
$$
Since an angular scale $\theta$ maps onto a multipole $\ell \sim \pi/\theta\,\mathrm{rad} \sim 180^\circ/\theta$, this corresponds to a characteristic multipole $\ell_H \sim 200$.
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
    \vspace{-2em}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/cmb-tt.png}
    \captionof{figure}{CMB TT power spectrum.}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Modes with $\ell \lesssim 200$ were therefore still \textbf{super-horizon} at the time of decoupling: causal microphysics (in particular, the acoustic oscillations) had not yet had time to act on them.

On super-horizon scales, only two effects can source a non-zero temperature fluctuation $\Delta T/T$: the intrinsic photon-density fluctuation present at decoupling, and the gravitational redshift (or blueshift) a photon experiences climbing out of (or into) a potential well, since photons emerging from a deeper potential well lose more energy than the mean. The combination of these two effects is known as the (ordinary) \textbf{Sachs-Wolfe effect}, and it sets the low-$\ell$ plateau of the temperature power spectrum, together with part of the normalisation of the first acoustic peak.

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsubsection{Acoustic Oscillations and the Peak Structure} \label{subsec:acoustic_peaks_detail}

On sub-horizon scales, by contrast, the rich peak structure of the temperature spectrum is a direct consequence of \bfit{acoustic oscillations} in the tightly coupled baryon-photon fluid prior to recombination. Gravitational potential wells, sourced predominantly by the dominant cold dark matter component, drive the compression of the fluid, while photon pressure, arising from Thomson scattering off free electrons, supplies the restoring force; the system behaves as a driven, damped harmonic oscillator, with a Hubble drag term and a pressure (sound-speed) term entering alongside the gravitational driving term.

\smallskip

Because the sound speed of the photon-baryon fluid is common to all Fourier modes, shorter-wavelength modes complete more oscillation cycles by the time of recombination than longer-wavelength ones. The \textbf{first peak} corresponds to the mode caught at its first compression at the moment of recombination; the \textbf{second peak} to the mode that has completed a full cycle of compression and rarefaction; and so on for the higher peaks. The even-numbered peaks are systematically suppressed relative to the odd ones, because the rarefaction phase must work against the inertia of the baryons, which do not participate in the restoring pressure force but do add to the inertial mass of the oscillating fluid.

\smallskip

% After recombination, photons free-stream toward the observer largely unimpeded, carrying the imprint of the oscillation phase of each mode at the moment it was "frozen in". A single Fourier mode, viewed on the last-scattering surface, appears to the observer as a ring of angular temperature fluctuations with a characteristic scale set by the mode's wavelength; the ensemble of all such modes, summed over the sky, produces the full acoustic peak structure observed in $C_\ell$.

% \smallskip

On sufficiently small scales, two effects act together to erase power. First, because photons are not completely frozen, but are able to scatter, this random walk washes out density fluctuations on scales comparable to or smaller than the diffusion length, an effect known as \bfit{Silk damping}. Second, because the LSS has a finite thickness ($\Delta z \simeq 80$, \cref{subsec:lss_revisited}), anisotropies on angular scales smaller than the corresponding width are smeared out by projection along the line of sight. Both effects act above $\ell \gtrsim 1000$, producing the characteristic exponential suppression of power known as the \textbf{damping tail}.

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsubsection{Secondary Anisotropies} \label{subsec:secondary_anisotropies_detail}

\begin{minipage}{0.57\textwidth}

At angular scales below a few arcminutes, the observed temperature fluctuations are no longer dominated by the primary physics of the last-scattering surface, but instead by \bfit{secondary anisotropies}: distortions imprinted on the CMB by its subsequent interaction with matter along the line of sight. These fall into two broad families, distinguished by the underlying physical mechanism.

\smallskip

The first family comes from the interaction of photons with gravitational potential wells, mainly gravitational lensing (\cref{sec:lensing_probe}) and the \textbf{late Integrated Sachs-Wolfe (ISW) effect}, which appears when gravitational potentials decay after dark energy dominates. Lensing by cosmic structures smooths the acoustic peaks (up to $10\%$ at $\ell > 2000$) and boosts small-scale power, overtaking the primary anisotropies for $\ell > 4000$. Lensing also correlates otherwise independent Fourier modes, introducing a specific non-Gaussian signature in the four-point statistics, allowing reconstruction of the lensing potential (\cref{subsec:cmb_lensing_probe}).

\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \vspace{-1.5em}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/sec-ani.png}
    
    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Secondary CMB anisotropies, caused by gravitational (a) and scattering (b) effects.}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

The second family incorporates the effects of photons rescattering off free electrons after recombination: the reionization signature already discussed in \cref{sec:reionization}, and the thermal and kinetic SZ effects of \cref{sec:icm_mm}.

\subsubsection{Spectrum decomposition} \label{subsubsec:spectrum_decomposition}

The observed temperature power spectrum can be usefully decomposed into the sum of several physically distinct contributions: the Sachs-Wolfe and early-ISW terms, which dominate the largest scales and set the low-$\ell$ plateau together with part of the first-peak normalisation; the acoustic oscillation terms proper, which produce the peak-trough sequence; the late-ISW contribution, adding power at the very lowest multipoles from the decay of potentials at late times; a Doppler (velocity) term, contributing around the acoustic region with a phase offset relative to the density term; and the diffusion-damping suppression at high $\ell$ discussed above.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.38\textwidth}
    The \textbf{position} of the acoustic peaks is set by two purely geometric quantities: the \bfit{physical size} of the sound horizon at decoupling, and the \bfit{angular diameter distance} to the last-scattering surface. Peak positions therefore encode $\Omega_b h^2$, $\Omega_m h^2$, the spatial curvature $\Omega_k$, and the dark-energy content through its effect on the distance-redshift relation; departures from flatness shift the whole peak pattern horizontally in $\ell$, toward smaller $\ell$ for open models and larger $\ell$ for closed ones.

    \smallskip

\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/cmb-tt-decomposed.png}
    
    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Breakdown of the CMB temperature power spectrum into physical components.}
\end{minipage}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
    The \textbf{relative heights} of the peaks, by contrast, carry complementary information. Increasing $\Omega_b h^2$ \bfit{enhances the  relative amplitude} of odd peaks to the even ones (the \bfit{baryon-loading effect} already discussed) and deepens the intervening troughs. The overall envelope of the peak heights is additionally sensitive to the primordial scalar amplitude and tilt ($A_s$, $n_s$) and to the time evolution of the gravitational potentials, through the early- and late-ISW terms. 

    \smallskip

    In short, the CMB temperature spectrum constrains the Universe's \textit{geometry} chiefly through the peak positions, and its \textit{composition and dynamics} chiefly through the relative peak heights and the damping tail.
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.41\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{assets/cmb-tt-probe.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

\end{minipage}

\subsection{CMB Polarisation Spectra: EE and TE} \label{subsec:ee_te_detail}

\cref{subsec:cmb_polarization} introduced the generation of CMB polarisation via Thomson scattering off a local quadrupole anisotropy in the radiation field, and the resulting $E$/$B$-mode decomposition. Beyond temperature, polarisation yields two additional two-point observables, $C_\ell^{EE}$ and $C_\ell^{TE}$.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.59\textwidth}
    Because polarisation is most efficiently produced on scales comparable to the photon diffusion length near decoupling, the $EE$ spectrum peaks around $\ell \sim 1000$, at an amplitude only a few percent of the temperature fluctuations (few $\mu\text{K}$). A second, much fainter feature appears at low $\ell$: the \textbf{reionisation bump}, sourced by the re-scattering of CMB photons off the free electrons liberated during reionisation (\cref{sec:reionization}); its amplitude is set by the (low) probability of a recent scattering, and its angular position by the size of the horizon at the epoch of reionisation.
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.38\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/cmb-pol-signal.png}
    
    \vspace{-0.5em}
    
    \captionof{figure}{CMB power spectra: TT, EE, TE, and BB components.}
\end{minipage}

\smallskip

CMB polarisation is produced by the local \textit{velocity} of the photon-baryon fluid at last scattering, because the polarising quadrupole arises from anisotropic photon motion, not directly from density or pressure. In contrast, temperature anisotropies mainly trace density extrema. This causes the $EE$ and $TE$ peaks to be shifted by a quarter cycle relative to the $TT$ peaks. The $TE$ cross-spectrum, which flips sign across multipoles due to this phase shift, is particularly useful for breaking parameter degeneracies left by $TT$ alone.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.6\textwidth}
    Modern CMB experiments like Planck, SPT, and ACT now provide the strongest constraints on $\Lambda$CDM parameters, thanks to their precise measurements of both temperature and polarisation anisotropies. All-sky surveys such as Planck reach the limit set by cosmic variance at large angular scales (low $\ell$), with the $TT$ spectrum already cosmic-variance limited for $\ell \lesssim 1500$ (\cref{subsubsec:cmb_powerspectrum}). Further improvement comes from high-resolution polarisation data and by combining experiments with different strengths, merging Planck's full-sky coverage with the finer angular detail of SPT or ACT. These joint analyses increase the accessible multipole range and tighten parameter constraints, as shown in \cref{fig:cmb_param_constraints}, which compares posteriors from different dataset combinations.
\end{minipage}

\subsection{The tSZ Angular Power Spectrum} \label{subsec:tsz_power_spectrum_detail}

Beyond counting individual clusters (\cref{subsec:tsz_secondary}), the angular power spectrum of the full Compton-$y$ map, $C_\ell^{\text{tSZ}}$, provides a sensitive cosmological probe. On large scales,
$$
C_\ell^{\text{tSZ}} \propto \sigma_8^{8.1}\,\Omega_m^{3.2}\,B^{-3.2}\,h^{-1.7} ,
$$
where $B$ is the hydrostatic mass bias (\cref{subsec:hse}). The strong dependence on $\sigma_8$ makes the tSZ power spectrum a powerful probe of structure growth, but large degeneracies with $\Omega_m$ and $B$ makes it mainly constrain combinations as $F \equiv \sigma_8\,(\Omega_m/B)^{0.4}\,h^{-0.21}$, unless bias is externally calibrated.

\medskip

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{assets/tsz-pws.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{tSZ angular power spectrum sensitivity to cosmological and astrophysical parameters.}
\end{center}

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.72\textwidth}
    Because the tSZ signal is sourced by all collapsed halos along the line of sight, its angular power spectrum can be accurately modeled using the halo model. This naturally splits the total power into two terms:
    \medskip
    $$
    C_\ell^{\mathrm{tSZ}} = C_\ell^{\mathrm{1\text{-}halo}} + C_\ell^{\mathrm{2\text{-}halo}}
    $$

    The \textbf{1-halo term} captures the power arising from small scales (the internal structure of individual halos) by integrating over the pressure profiles of all clusters and groups. The \textbf{2-halo term} accounts for correlations between separate halos on large scales:
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.25\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/halo-terms.png}
    \captionof{figure}{}
\end{minipage}

$$
\begin{cases}
    \displaystyle C_\ell^{\mathrm{1\text{-}halo}} = \int_0^{z_\mathrm{max}} dz\, \frac{dV_c}{dz\,d\Omega} \int_{M_\mathrm{min}}^{M_\mathrm{max}} dM\, \frac{dn(M, z)}{dM}\; \left|\tilde{y}_\ell(M, z)\right|^2\\
    \displaystyle C_\ell^{\mathrm{2\text{-}halo}} = \int_0^{z_\mathrm{max}} dz\, \frac{dV_c}{dz\,d\Omega} \times \left[ \int_{M_\mathrm{min}}^{M_\mathrm{max}} dM\, \frac{dn(M, z)}{dM}\; \tilde{y}_\ell(M, z)\, b(M, z) \right]^2 P(k,z)
\end{cases}
$$

Here, $dV_c/dzd\Omega$ is the comoving volume element, $dn/dM$ is the halo mass function, $\tilde{y}_\ell(M, z)$ is the Fourier transform of the Compton-$y$ profile of a halo of mass $M$ at redshift $z$, $b(M, z)$ is the halo bias, and $P(k, z)$ is the linear matter power spectrum at wavenumber $k = \ell / D_A(z)$. 

\section{Gravitational Lensing as a Cosmological Probe} \label{sec:lensing_probe}

As light from a distant source travels toward us, it is deflected by every gravitational potential fluctuation it crosses. This \bfit{gravitational lensing} leaves three observable imprints: a shift in the apparent position of the source, a distortion (shear) of its image, and a magnification of its apparent flux. Two regimes are conventionally distinguished. \textbf{Strong lensing}, occurring where the deflection is large (typically in the dense core of a cluster or the potential of an individual galaxy), can produce multiple images of the same source, along with strong distortion and magnification. \textbf{Weak lensing}, occurring everywhere else, produces only a small, statistical distortion of individual source shapes and must be recovered by averaging over large numbers of background sources.

\subsection{The Lens Equation} \label{subsec:lens_equation}

Consider an observer, a lens at comoving distance $\chi_1$, and a source at comoving distance $\chi$, with corresponding angular diameter distances $D(\chi_1)$, $D(\chi)$, and $D(\chi-\chi_1)$ between lens and source. 

\medskip

\begin{center}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/lens-eq.png}
    \captionof{figure}{Geometry of gravitational lensing.}
\end{center}

\medskip

The observer sees the source at an apparent angular position $\vec\theta$, while its true, undeflected position is $\vec\beta$. Working in the small-angle approximation (valid for essentially all astrophysical lensing configurations), the physical position of the source in the source plane can be written as:
\medskip
$$
\vec\beta\,D(\chi) = \vec\theta\,D(\chi) - \vec\alpha\,D(\chi-\chi_1)
$$
where $\vec\alpha$ is the physical bending angle at the lens plane. This rearranges into the \bfit{lens equation}:
\medskip
$$
\vec\beta = \vec\theta - \delta\vec\theta , \qquad
\delta\vec\theta = \frac{D(\chi-\chi_1)}{D(\chi)}\,\vec\alpha
$$
where $\delta\vec\theta$ is the scaled deflection angle and the distance ratio encodes the lensing geometry of the observer-lens-source configuration. The deflection itself follows from treating the light path in the weak-field, perturbed FRW metric,
\medskip
$$
ds^2 = \left(1+\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right)c^2\,dt^2 - a^2(t)\left(1-\frac{2\Phi}{c^2}\right)\left(d\chi^2 + D^2(\chi)\,d\Omega^2\right)
$$
in which the potential $\Phi$ plays the role of a spatially varying refractive index. Under the further \textbf{thin-lens} and \textbf{Born} approximations (the deflection is treated as occurring instantaneously at the lens plane, evaluated along the unperturbed line of sight), the physical bending angle is:
\medskip
$$
\vec\alpha = \frac{2}{c^2}\int \nabla_\perp \Phi(\chi)\,d\chi
$$
where $\nabla_\perp \Phi$ is the transverse gradient of the gravitational potential.

\begin{minipage}{0.62\textwidth}
    As light propagate through the Universe, it is continually deflected by the gravitational potentials of intervening matter fluctuations. Thus, the cumulative deflection angle is:
    $$
    \delta\vec\theta = \vec\theta - \vec\beta = \frac{2}{c^2}\int_0^{\chi_s} d\chi\;\frac{D(\chi_s-\chi)}{D(\chi_s)}\,\nabla_\perp \Phi(\chi)
    $$
    It is convenient to represent this deflection field as the gradient of a scalar \textbf{deflection potential}:
    $$
    \psi(\vec\theta,\chi_s) = \frac{2}{c^2}\int_0^{\chi_s} d\chi\;\frac{D(\chi_s-\chi)}{D(\chi_s)\,D(\chi)}\,\Phi\big(D(\chi)\,\vec\theta,\,\chi\big)
    $$
    This quantity projects the three-dimensional gravitational potential $\Phi$ along the line of sight, weighted by a lensing kernel determined by the source and lens geometry.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.36\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{assets/wl-path.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Gravitational lensing bends light from distant sources, distorting their observed shapes.}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{-0.5em}

\subsection{Cosmic Shear} \label{subsec:cosmic_shear_detail}

\subsubsection{Convergence and Shear} \label{subsubsec:convergence_shear}

Gravitational lensing has the important property of conserving surface brightness, meaning that while the shapes of images may be distorted and their sizes magnified or demagnified, the intrinsic brightness per unit solid angle remains unchanged. As a result, the observed image intensity at angular position $\vec\theta$ is related to the intrinsic (source plane) intensity by remapping positions according to the lens equation:
$$
I(\vec\theta) = I^s(\vec\beta(\vec\theta))
$$

To quantify how lensing distorts the shapes and orientations of infinitesimal images, we linearise this mapping around a chosen line of sight $I(\vec\theta)= \left[I^s(\vec\beta(\vec\theta_0)) + \mathcal A (\vec \theta_0) \cdot (\vec \theta - \vec \theta_0)\right]$. This leads naturally to the definition of the \bfit{Jacobian (amplification) matrix}, which describes how a small patch in the image plane is mapped to the source plane:
$$
\mathcal{A}_{ij} = \frac{\partial\beta_i}{\partial\theta_j} = \delta_{ij} - \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial\theta_i\,\partial\theta_j} =
\begin{pmatrix}
1-\kappa-\gamma_1 & -\gamma_2 \\
-\gamma_2 & 1-\kappa+\gamma_1
\end{pmatrix}
 = \left(1-\kappa\right)
\begin{pmatrix}
1-g_1 & -g_2 \\
-g_2 & 1+g_1
\end{pmatrix}
$$
where $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta, $\psi$ is the deflection potential defined above, and $g(\vec\theta) \equiv \frac{\gamma(\vec\theta)}{1-\kappa(\vec\theta)}$ is the \textbf{reduced shear}. The second derivatives of the deflection potential can be decomposed into two distinct physical quantities. The \textbf{convergence} $\kappa$ is an isotropic magnification (or demagnification) of the image, proportional to the projected surface mass density along the line of sight:
$$
\kappa = \frac{\psi_{,11}+\psi_{,22}}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\nabla^2\psi
$$
 The \textbf{shear} $\gamma$, is a complex quantity describing the anisotropic stretching of the image:
$$
\gamma = \gamma_1 + i\gamma_2 = |\gamma|\,e^{2i\varphi} , \qquad
\gamma_1 = \frac{\psi_{,11}-\psi_{,22}}{2} , \qquad \gamma_2 = \psi_{,12}
$$
which is a spin-2 field (invariant under $180^\circ$ rotation) and can be described by modulus and phase.

\begin{minipage}{0.41\textwidth}
\smallskip

    A circular source of radius $R$ is thus mapped, to linear order, into an ellipse of semi-axes $R/(1-\kappa-|\gamma|)$ and $R/(1-\kappa+|\gamma|)$. Convergence alone rescales the image isotropically, while shear stretches it along a preferred axis set by $\varphi$.
    \medskip
\end{minipage}%

\subsubsection{Cosmic Shear} \label{subsubsec:cosmic_shear_detail}

The convergence field along a given line of sight is itself a projection of the intervening matter density contrast,
$$
\kappa(\vec\theta) = \int_0^{\chi_{\max}} d\chi\;W(\chi)\,\delta\big(D_A(\chi)\vec\theta,\,\chi\big) ,
$$
where the \textbf{lensing efficiency kernel} $W(\chi)$ depends on the redshift distribution of the source population and the observer-lens-source geometry. The corresponding angular convergence (equivalently, shear) power spectrum follows from Limber's approximation as a line-of-sight integral over the three-dimensional matter power spectrum,
$$
C_{ij}(\ell) = \int_0^{\chi_{\max}} d\chi\;\frac{W_i(\chi)\,W_j(\chi)}{D_A(\chi)^2}\,P_\delta\!\left(\frac{\ell}{D_A(\chi)},\,\chi\right) ,
$$
where the indices $i,j$ label pairs of source redshift bins (tomographic slices). 

\medskip

This \textbf{cosmic shear} signal, the coherent, statistical distortion of background galaxy shapes by the intervening large-scale structure, has the considerable virtue of tracing the total matter distribution directly, without any assumption about the relation between dark and baryonic matter; combined with its sensitivity to both geometry and the growth of structure, this makes it a powerful, largely model-independent probe of dark energy and of modified gravity.

\smallskip

\begin{minipage}{0.59\textwidth}
In practice, the shear signal is measured statistically by correlating galaxy ellipticities in pairs. Decomposing the shear at each galaxy into components tangential and orthogonal ("cross") to the line joining a pair:
\medskip
$$
\gamma_t = -\mathfrak{Re}\!\left[\gamma\,e^{-2i\varphi}\right] , \qquad \gamma_\times = -\mathfrak{Im}\!\left[\gamma\,e^{-2i\varphi}\right] ,
$$
one constructs the rotationally invariant two-point shear correlation functions:
\medskip
$$
\begin{array}{rl}
    \xi_\pm(\theta) &= \langle\gamma_t\gamma_t\rangle(\theta) \pm \langle\gamma_\times\gamma_\times\rangle(\theta) \\[0.5em]
    \xi_\times(\theta) &= \langle\gamma_t\gamma_\times\rangle(\theta) \stackrel{!}{=} 0
\end{array}
$$
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.38\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/shear.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Shear geometry.}
\end{minipage}

related to the convergence power spectrum via a Hankel transform, $\xi_+(\theta) = \int_0^\infty \frac{d\ell\,\ell}{2\pi}\,J_0(\ell\theta)\,P_\kappa(\ell)$. Under parity symmetry, the cross-correlation $\xi_\times(\theta)$ must vanish identically, providing a useful null test for residual systematics in real data.


\smallskip

Cosmic shear surveys do not measure the shear $\gamma$ directly, but rather the total observed ellipticity $\epsilon$ of each galaxy, which combines the intrinsic (pre-lensing) ellipticity $\epsilon^s$ and the lensing-induced shear: $\epsilon = \epsilon^s + \gamma$. The main observable (the two-point ellipticity correlation) receives contributions from several effects. For galaxies $i$ and $j$ ($z_i < z_j$), this can be written schematically as:
$$
\xi_\pm = \underbrace{\langle \epsilon_i^{\text{lens}} \epsilon_j^{\text{lens}*} \rangle}_{\text{Cosmic shear signal}} + \underbrace{\langle \epsilon_i^{(s)} \epsilon_j^{(s)*} \rangle}_{\text{Intrinsic alignment}} + \underbrace{\langle \gamma_i \epsilon_j^{(s)*} \rangle}_{\text{Shape-shear}} + \underbrace{\langle \epsilon_i^{(s)} \gamma_j^* \rangle}_{\text{Shape-shear}}
$$

\vspace{-1em}

where
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \bfit{cosmic shear} term is the genuine lensing correlation between galaxies at different redshifts, produced by large-scale structure along the line of sight.
    \item The \bfit{intrinsic alignment} term, $\langle \epsilon_i^{(s)} \epsilon_j^{(s)*} \rangle$, arises because the intrinsic shapes of galaxies physically close together can be mutually aligned by the tidal field of the same dark matter structure.
    \item The \bfit{shape-shear} term, $\langle \gamma_i \epsilon_j^{(s)*} \rangle + \langle \epsilon_i^{(s)} \gamma_j^* \rangle$, describes the correlation between the intrinsic shape of a foreground galaxy and the lensing shear of a background one, both sourced by the same large-scale structure.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Measuring shapes in practice}

The observed image of a galaxy is shaped by several sequential effects, commonly called the “forward” process:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Each galaxy starts with an unknown intrinsic structure, which is then distorted by weak lensing shear $g$ (typically $g\sim 0.03$), causing a slight stretch, much smaller than the galaxy's natural ellipticity.

\item The lensed image is blurred by the instrument's \textbf{point spread function} (PSF), reflecting optical imperfections, detector response, and atmospheric turbulence for ground-based telescopes. The PSF typically broadens the image, often to a size comparable to the galaxy itself.

\item Finally, the image is recorded by the detector, becoming pixelated and subject to shot noise and read noise.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting observed image is therefore both blurrier and noisier than the original.

\smallskip

\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{assets/forward.png}

    \vspace{-0.5em}

    \captionof{figure}{Forward process: Galaxy images are sheared by lensing, blurred by the PSF, pixelated, and made noisy; stars, as point sources, are affected only by the PSF and noise.}
\end{center}

\smallskip

Recovering weak lensing shear requires astronomers to statistically invert each step of the image formation process. The most critical and difficult part is \bfit{PSF correction}: the true pre-lensed galaxy shape is unknown, so the PSF must be inferred from point sources (stars) in the same field. The PSF is measured from the observed shapes of these stars (since they are effectively point sources) and then deconvolved from the galaxy images. Any PSF modeling or removal error can mimic or hide the subtle shear signal. Because cosmic shear is much smaller than the shape noise of individual galaxies, reliable measurement demands averaging over huge samples and strict control of systematic errors.

\subsection{Cosmic Shear Cosmology}

Cosmic shear was first detected in 2000 by four independent groups, using samples of order $10^4$-$10^5$ galaxies over roughly $1\,\text{deg}^2$; current surveys such as DES and KiDS achieve samples exceeding $10^8$ galaxies over several thousand square degrees, yielding constraints in the $\Omega_m$-$\sigma_8$ plane that are competitive with, and complementary to, those from the primary CMB.

\subsubsection{CMB Lensing} \label{subsec:cmb_lensing_probe}

The CMB itself, originating from the last-scattering surface at $z\simeq1100$, acts as a single, very high-redshift background source, gravitationally lensed by every intervening structure along the line of sight. The lensed temperature field $T(\hat n)$ is related to the unlensed field $\tilde T(\hat n)$ by a remapping through the deflection field $\vec\alpha = \nabla\phi$:
\medskip
$$
T(\hat n) = \tilde T(\hat n + \nabla\phi(\hat n)) \approx \tilde T(\hat n) + \nabla\phi(\hat n)\cdot\nabla\tilde T(\hat n) ,
$$
where $\phi$ is the CMB lensing potential (analogue of $\psi$ in \cref{subsec:lens_equation}, for a source at the last-scattering surface). Because this remapping locally rescales the size of hot and cold spots, it mixes Fourier modes that would otherwise be statistically independent in an unlensed, isotropic sky:
\medskip
$$
\langle T(\vec\ell_1)\,T(\vec\ell_2)\rangle_{\text{fixed}\,\phi} \approx f(\vec\ell_1,\vec\ell_2)\,\phi(\vec\ell_1+\vec\ell_2) ,
$$
where $f$ is a weight function chosen to maximise the signal-to-noise of the reconstruction. By measuring the resulting correlation between small-scale temperature (and polarisation) fluctuations and the large-scale CMB gradient across the sky, the lensing potential $\phi$, and hence the deflection field, can be reconstructed statistically.

\medskip


\begin{minipage}{0.57\textwidth}
    The lensing potential $\phi$ is an integrated measure of all gravitational potential fluctuations encountered by CMB photons on their journey from the last-scattering surface to us. Its angular power spectrum,
    \medskip
    $$
    C_\ell^{\phi\phi} = \frac{4}{\ell^4}\int_0^{\chi_*} \frac{W^2(\chi)}{\chi^2}\,P_m(k, z)\,d\chi
    $$
    serves as a powerful cosmological observable derived from the CMB alone.
    
    \smallskip
    
    This spectrum captures both the late-time expansion history of the Universe and the growth of large-scale structure, providing information that is only weakly or degenerately accessible from the primary temperature and polarization anisotropies.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill

\medskip

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Amplitude}: Measures the total matter content and the amplitude of density fluctuations (constraining parameters like $\Omega_m$, $\sigma_8$).
        \item \textbf{Shape}: Tracks how structure grows with time, making it sensitive to the summed neutrino mass and the properties of dark energy.
    \end{itemize}

The remarkable sensitivity of the CMB lensing power spectrum's amplitude and shape now allows leading experiments such as Planck, ACT, and SPT (separately and in combination) to provide increasingly precise measurements.

\section{Multi-Probe Cosmology and Cross-Correlations} \label{sec:multiprobe}

We have seen many cosmological probes: CMB, cluster abundance, cosmic shear, galaxy clustering, and standard candles and rulers. In recent years, combining these probes, rather than single analysis, has been shown to significantly improve cosmological constraints.

\subsection{Cross-Correlations Between Tracers} \label{subsec:cross_correlation}

Combining different cosmological probes is so powerful because the tracers (such as galaxy density, lensing, and clusters) all map the same underlying matter field, and thus are statistically correlated. Where the density of galaxies is high, lensing distortions of background sources will also be larger. These shared fluctuations induce \textbf{cross-correlations} that contain additional information beyond what each probe offers alone. For two tracers $a$ and $b$, their cross angular power spectrum is defined as:
\medskip
$$
\langle a_{\ell m}\,b^*_{\ell' m'}\rangle = C_\ell^{ab}\,\delta_{\ell\ell'}\,\delta_{mm'} , \qquad
C_\ell^{ab} = 4\pi\int_0^\infty \frac{dk}{k}\,\mathcal{P}_\Phi(k)\,\Delta_\ell^a(k)\,\Delta_\ell^b(k) ,
$$
where $\mathcal{P}_\Phi(k)$ is the primordial matter power spectrum, and $\Delta_\ell^{a}(k)$, $\Delta_\ell^{b}(k)$ are the transfer functions describing how primordial fluctuations seed the tracers $a$ and $b$. By measuring these cross-correlations, we gain extra cosmological information and can better self-calibrate key nuisance effects (like galaxy bias or photometric redshift uncertainty) that affect the individual probes.

\subsection{The 3x2pt and 6x2pt+N Analyses} \label{subsec:3x2pt}

A widely used approach to multi-probe cosmology combines three two-point functions from galaxy positions and shapes: \textbf{angular clustering} (galaxy $\times$ galaxy), \textbf{galaxy-galaxy lensing} (galaxy $\times$ shear), and \textbf{cosmic shear} (shear $\times$ shear). This “\bfit{3x2pt}” analysis is standard in modern photometric surveys like DES and KiDS, as all three are affected by the same galaxy bias and lensing systematics, allowing these nuisance parameters to be jointly constrained with cosmology, rather than assumed. The resulting limits on $\Omega_m$ and $\sigma_8$ enhance the ones from single analysis.

\smallskip

These analyses can be further strengthened by including the abundance and clustering of galaxy clusters, as well as their auto- and cross-correlations with galaxies and shear. Depending on which observables and cluster counts $N$ are included, these extensions are called \bfit{4x2pt+N} or \bfit{6x2pt+N}. Because clusters trace the high-mass, highly biased end of the halo mass function, they add complementary information, though at the cost of greater model and covariance complexity. Applied to DES and other datasets, such analyses produce consistent, even tighter $\Omega_m$-$\sigma_8$ constraints, and offer an important cross-check on the mild $S_8$ tension between early- and late-Universe probes.
