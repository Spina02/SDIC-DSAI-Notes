\chapter{The Cosmic Microwave Background} \label{ch:cmb}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:cmb_intro}

\subsubsection{Historical Overview} \label{subsec:cmb_history}

The existence of the \bfit{cosmic microwave background} (CMB) was first predicted on theoretical grounds in 1948 by Gamow, Alpher, and Herman: if the Universe began in a hot, dense state, the intense thermal radiation from that early epoch should still permeate space today, redshifted by cosmic expansion into the microwave band. The prediction was confirmed sixteen years later, in 1964, when Penzias and Wilson, using a horn antenna at Bell Labs originally intended for satellite communication, detected a persistent, isotropic excess noise that could not be attributed to any instrumental or terrestrial source; this serendipitous discovery, recognised with the 1978 Nobel Prize, provided the decisive evidence in favour of the Big Bang model over the competing Steady State theory. Subsequent missions progressively sharpened the picture: \textbf{COBE} (1989) performed the first all-sky survey and revealed that the CMB is not perfectly smooth; \textbf{BOOMERanG} (1998), a stratospheric balloon experiment launched from Antarctica, first resolved the acoustic peaks in the temperature power spectrum; \textbf{WMAP} (2001) achieved a substantial improvement in sensitivity and angular resolution and reported the first full-sky detection of $E$-mode polarisation; and \textbf{Planck} (2009) delivered the most detailed all-sky map of the CMB to date, in both intensity and polarisation.

\subsection{The CMB Monopole} \label{subsec:cmb_monopole}

\begin{minipage}{0.52\textwidth}
The CMB spectrum is, to extraordinary precision, that of a perfect blackbody at a temperature $T = 2.725\,\text{K}$. This remarkable uniformity is itself powerful evidence for a hot, thermalised early Universe: any significant late-time energy injection would have distorted the spectrum away from a pure Planck function, and no such distortion has ever been measured.
\end{minipage}%

\subsection{The CMB Dipole} \label{subsec:cmb_dipole}

Superposed on the monopole, at the much smaller level of $\Delta T/T \sim 10^{-3}$ (an amplitude of order $3.35\,\text{mK}$), is a dipole pattern: one hemisphere of the sky appears systematically hotter, the opposite one systematically colder. This is a Doppler effect arising from our own peculiar motion through space, which sums the orbital motion of the Earth around the Sun ($\sim 30\,\text{km/s}$), the rotation of the Sun about the Galactic centre ($\sim 220\,\text{km/s}$), and the net motion of the Milky Way toward the Virgo cluster ($\sim 300\,\text{km/s}$); the vector sum of these contributions yields a net peculiar velocity of ${\sim}\,369\,\text{km/s}$. Because the dipole is purely kinematic in origin, it is conventionally subtracted before the intrinsic CMB anisotropies are analysed.

\section{CMB Anisotropies} \label{sec:cmb_anisotropies}

Once the monopole and dipole are removed, the CMB reveals a further layer of structure: tiny temperature fluctuations of order $\Delta T/T \sim 10^{-5}$, remarkably uniform in amplitude across the sky. These fluctuations originate as quantum fluctuations generated during inflation, stretched to cosmological scales and imprinted as the initial density perturbations of the Universe. Prior to \bfit{recombination}, the tightly coupled baryon-photon fluid resists gravitational collapse through radiation pressure, and the two effects together drive \bfit{acoustic oscillations}: overdense regions collapse under gravity, are halted and rebound under radiation pressure, and the fluid rings much like a sound wave. Once the Universe cools sufficiently for neutral hydrogen to form ($z \simeq 1100$), photons decouple from the baryons and begin to free-stream across the cosmos largely unimpeded. The temperature pattern observed today is therefore a direct snapshot of the density perturbations frozen in at the moment of recombination, the so-called \textbf{last scattering surface}.

\subsubsection{The Angular Power Spectrum} \label{subsubsec:cmb_powerspectrum}

To analyse the anisotropies statistically, the fractional temperature fluctuation
$$
\frac{\Delta T}{T}(\theta,\phi) = \frac{T(\theta,\phi) - \bar{T}}{\bar{T}}
$$
is expanded in spherical harmonics,
$$
\frac{\Delta T}{T}(\theta,\phi) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} a_{\ell m}\, Y_{\ell m}(\theta,\phi) ,
$$
where larger coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ correspond to a stronger contribution from the corresponding mode $Y_{\ell m}$. Because the individual phases carry no physical information for a statistically isotropic random field, the relevant observable is the \bfit{angular power spectrum},
$$
C_\ell = \frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} \langle |a_{\ell m}|^2 \rangle .
$$
Low multipoles $\ell$ correspond to large angular scales, and increasingly high $\ell$ probes progressively finer angular detail; the sequence of acoustic peaks visible in $C_\ell$ (\cref{fig:cmb_powerspectrum}) directly encodes the oscillation history of the photon-baryon fluid at recombination, and their positions and relative heights constrain essentially all of the fundamental cosmological parameters.

\begin{observationblock}[Cosmic variance and the limits of temperature measurements]
Because we have access to only a single realisation of the Universe, the temperature power spectrum is fundamentally \bfit{sample-variance limited}: the finite number of independent modes available at each $\ell$, $(2\ell+1)$, sets an irreducible statistical uncertainty
$$
\frac{\Delta C_\ell}{C_\ell} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{2\ell+1}} .
$$
This uncertainty is largest at low $\ell$ (large angular scales), where only a handful of independent modes exist, and shrinks toward higher $\ell$. Over essentially the entire range of scales that carry useful cosmological information (up to $\ell \simeq 1500$), Planck's temperature detectors were already sensitive enough that cosmic variance, rather than instrumental noise, was the dominant source of uncertainty. Building a more sensitive temperature satellite would therefore yield little further improvement in cosmological constraints; genuine gains instead require exploiting the polarisation of the CMB, whose signal has not yet reached the sample-variance floor at all scales.
\end{observationblock}

\subsection{CMB Polarisation} \label{subsec:cmb_polarization}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
Polarisation in the CMB arises from Thomson scattering. Consider a free electron illuminated by radiation from several directions: the electron is accelerated along the direction of the incident electric field and re-emits a new photon that is linearly polarised. If the incoming radiation is isotropic, contributions from perpendicular directions cancel and no net polarisation results. A net linear polarisation (at the level of ${\sim}\,10\%$) is produced only when the incident radiation field has a \bfit{quadrupole anisotropy}, that is, when the intensity varies systematically between directions separated by $90^\circ$; the resulting polarisation is aligned with the cold axis of the quadrupole.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}

    \label{fig:thomson_polarization}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

The epoch at which this quadrupole can actually develop is narrowly constrained: before recombination, scattering is far too frequent to allow any significant quadrupole to build up, while after recombination scatterings become extremely rare (until the Universe reionises, \cref{sec:reionization}). Recombination is therefore essentially the only epoch at which CMB polarisation is efficiently generated. Two distinct physical mechanisms can source the required quadrupole: scalar density fluctuations and tensor perturbations associated with a stochastic background of gravitational waves.

\smallskip

The polarisation observed today, projected across the sky, is conventionally decomposed into two orthogonal patterns. Because polarisation is a spin-2 field, this decomposition splits it into a curl-free component, the \bfit{$E$~mode} (polarisation aligned parallel or perpendicular to the wavevector, even parity, gradient-like), and a divergence-free component, the \bfit{$B$~mode} (polarisation tilted at $45^\circ$ to the wavevector, odd parity, curl-like). Crucially, scalar density perturbations produce only $E$-mode polarisation, whereas gravitational waves produce both $E$- and $B$-mode polarisation; this asymmetry makes $B$~modes a uniquely clean tracer of a primordial gravitational-wave background, and therefore of the inflationary epoch itself.

\smallskip

Observationally, the polarisation field is mapped through the \bfit{Stokes parameters} $Q$ and $U$, which give the local orientation of the electric field on the sky ($Q$ along the $0^\circ$-$90^\circ$ axis, $U$ along the $45^\circ$-$135^\circ$ axis). These are related to the $E$- and $B$-mode fields through
\begin{align*}
Q(\hat\theta) &= \int \frac{d^2\boldsymbol\ell}{(2\pi)^2}\, \bigl(E_{\boldsymbol\ell}\cos 2\phi_{\boldsymbol\ell} - B_{\boldsymbol\ell}\sin 2\phi_{\boldsymbol\ell}\bigr)\, e^{i\boldsymbol\ell\cdot\hat\theta} , \\
U(\hat\theta) &= \int \frac{d^2\boldsymbol\ell}{(2\pi)^2}\, \bigl(E_{\boldsymbol\ell}\sin 2\phi_{\boldsymbol\ell} + B_{\boldsymbol\ell}\cos 2\phi_{\boldsymbol\ell}\bigr)\, e^{i\boldsymbol\ell\cdot\hat\theta} ,
\end{align*}
with the total polarised intensity given by $P = \sqrt{Q^2+U^2}$.

\smallskip

Three angular power spectra can in principle be built from the polarisation and temperature fields: $\langle EE \rangle$, $\langle TE \rangle$, and $\langle BB \rangle$; the cross-spectra $\langle EB \rangle$ and $\langle TB \rangle$ vanish identically for parity-preserving fluctuations, since both change sign under a parity flip. All polarisation spectra are substantially weaker than the temperature signal: the $EE$ spectrum is noise-limited but reaches the sample-variance floor by $\ell \simeq 3000$, while the $BB$ spectrum remains noise-limited and, for its primordial component, still undetected.

\subsubsection{Foreground Contamination} \label{subsubsec:cmb_foregrounds}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
Extracting the CMB signal requires separating it from Galactic foregrounds, principally synchrotron emission (dominant at low frequencies) and thermal dust emission (dominant at high frequencies). In temperature, a narrow "sweet spot" between roughly $70$ and $100\,\text{GHz}$ exists where the CMB dominates over both foregrounds; observations at other frequencies can then be used to characterise and subtract the residual contamination. In polarisation, however, no such clean window exists: synchrotron and dust foregrounds remain comparable to, or brighter than, the CMB polarisation signal across nearly the entire frequency range accessible from the ground or from space, making multi-frequency component separation essential for any polarisation measurement.
\end{minipage}%

\subsubsection{The Search for Primordial $B$~modes} \label{subsubsec:cmb_bmodes}

Within the simplest single-field inflationary models, the amplitude of the primordial $BB$ power spectrum, peaking at degree scales ($\ell \sim 100$), is controlled by a single parameter, the \bfit{tensor-to-scalar ratio} $r$. Because no high angular resolution is required to probe these large scales, dedicated $B$-mode experiments favour large numbers of low-noise detectors over fine angular resolution. Early searches yielded only upper limits: BICEP1 set a $2\sigma$ bound of $r < 0.7$ in 2014.

\begin{observationblock}[The BICEP2 dust controversy]
\textbf{BICEP2} was purpose-built to detect primordial $B$~modes, operating at $150\,\text{GHz}$ near the peak of the CMB blackbody spectrum and targeting the "Southern Hole", a patch of sky then believed to be exceptionally free of Galactic dust and synchrotron contamination, with an expected foreground contribution of only $r < 0.01$. In 2014 the collaboration reported a striking excess of power in the $BB$ spectrum precisely where the inflationary gravitational-wave signal was expected to peak, inferring $r = 0.20^{+0.07}_{-0.05}$ and excluding $r=0$ at $7\sigma$.

\smallskip

Subsequent Planck dust maps at $353\,\text{GHz}$, unavailable at the time of the original analysis, revealed that polarised dust emission in the BICEP2 field was substantially brighter than the earlier models had assumed, dust being some $20\times$ brighter at $353\,\text{GHz}$ than at $150\,\text{GHz}$. A joint BICEP2/Keck and Planck analysis, exploiting this multi-frequency information to clean the dust contribution, revised the bound down to $r_{0.05} < 0.12$ at $95\%$ confidence, consistent with no primordial signal. The episode stands as a cautionary tale about inferring a cosmological detection from a single frequency channel, and simultaneously demonstrated the power of multi-frequency foreground cleaning: with Planck's dust maps included, $B$-mode constraints became competitive with those from the temperature spectrum itself.
\end{observationblock}

The most recent constraints, combining Planck, BICEP/Keck (through the 2018 season, "BK18"), and baryon acoustic oscillation and lensing data, place the bound at $r < 0.037$ ($2\sigma$), already sufficient to rule out several of the simplest, historically favoured single-field inflationary potentials. $B$-mode detection remains the primary science driver of the next generation of CMB experiments (CMB-S4, the Simons Observatory, LiteBIRD, described in \cref{sec:mm_cmb}), which aim to reach $\mu\text{K}$-arcmin sensitivity in polarisation over nearly the full sky.

\section{The Epoch of Reionization} \label{sec:reionization}

\subsection{Cosmic Timeline} \label{subsec:reionization_timeline}

After the last scattering epoch ($z \simeq 1100$, $t \simeq 380\,000\,\text{yr}$), the Universe enters the \bfit{Dark Ages}: matter is almost entirely neutral, and no astrophysical sources yet exist to emit ionising photons. The Dark Ages end with the formation of the first stars, at redshifts thought to lie around $z \sim 25$-$30$. As increasingly numerous sources of ultraviolet radiation switch on, isolated ionised bubbles form around them, grow, and eventually overlap, marking the \bfit{epoch of reionization} proper ($z \sim 6$-$15$); by $z \sim 6$ the intergalactic medium (IGM) is essentially fully ionised, and the subsequent Post-Reionization era is dominated by the assembly of galaxies observed today. Because the low-redshift Universe is strongly shaped by subsequent astrophysical (baryonic) processes, the reionization epoch offers a comparatively clean window onto the properties of the first luminous sources and the growth of large-scale structure.

\subsection{Evidence from the CMB} \label{subsec:reionization_cmb}

CMB photons that traverse the reionized IGM after last scattering have a non-negligible probability of Thomson-scattering off the newly liberated free electrons. The relevant observable is the \bfit{optical depth to reionization},
$$
\tau = \int_0^{z_{\text{re}}} n_e(z)\,\sigma_{\mathrm T}\, \frac{c\,dz}{(1+z)H(z)} ,
$$
where $n_e(z)$ is the free-electron density and $H(z)$ the Hubble parameter; because the IGM is essentially neutral at $z > z_{\text{re}}$, the integral effectively receives contributions only from the epoch of reionization itself, down to the present.

\smallskip

Since this additional scattering is a random process along the line of sight, it does not imprint new structure but rather isotropically damps the primary temperature fluctuations at all angular scales,
$$
C_\ell^{TT,\text{obs}} = e^{-2\tau}\, C_\ell^{TT,\text{primordial}} .
$$
This damping is, however, degenerate with the amplitude of the primordial density fluctuations (parametrised by $A_s$ or, equivalently, $\sigma_8$), since both suppress or enhance the observed spectrum in a very similar way,
$$
C_\ell^{TT,\text{obs}} \propto A_s\, e^{-2\tau} .
$$
The degeneracy can be broken using the temperature-polarisation cross-spectrum: reionization polarises CMB photons for the first time since recombination, producing a distinctive "bump" in the $TE$ spectrum at large scales ($\ell \lesssim 10$) that has no counterpart in $A_s$ alone.

\smallskip

Extracting $z_{\text{re}}$ in practice requires assuming a model for the reionization history $n_e(z)$. The simplest and most widely used is the \textbf{instantaneous reionization} model, in which the ionisation fraction transitions from neutral to fully ionised over a narrow redshift interval ($\Delta z_{\text{re}} \simeq 0.5$) following a symmetric hyperbolic-tangent profile,
$$
x_e(z) = \frac{1+f_{\text{He}}}{2}\left[1 + \tanh\!\left(\frac{(1+z_{\text{re}})^{3/2} - (1+z)^{3/2}}{\Delta w}\right)\right] .
$$
Current Planck constraints, under this simplified model, give $z_{\text{re}} \simeq 7.7$.

\subsection{The Lyman-$\alpha$ Forest} \label{subsec:lyman_alpha}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
Independent, and more direct, evidence for the ionisation state of the IGM comes from the spectra of distant quasars (QSOs). Any neutral hydrogen encountered by the QSO's radiation along the line of sight absorbs photons at the rest-frame Lyman-$\alpha$ wavelength, $\lambda_{\text{fi}} = 1216\,\text{\AA}$. Because the radiation redshifts continuously as the Universe expands, a photon emitted at the QSO with $\lambda_Q < \lambda_{\text{fi}}$ redshifts into resonance with any intervening neutral hydrogen cloud at an intermediate redshift $z < z_Q$, producing a distinct absorption feature. The condition for resonance at redshift $z$ is
$$
\frac{\nu_Q}{1+z_Q} = \frac{\nu_{\text{fi}}}{1+z} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \lambda_Q(1+z_Q) = \lambda_{\text{fi}}(1+z) .
$$
The dense forest of such absorption features shortward of the QSO's own Lyman-$\alpha$ emission line, produced by the many discrete clouds of neutral hydrogen along the line of sight, gives the phenomenon its name: the \bfit{Lyman-$\alpha$ forest}.
\end{minipage}%

\begin{exampleblock}[Redshift of a Lyman-$\alpha$ absorber]
Consider a QSO at $z_Q = 3$ emitting a photon at rest-frame wavelength $\lambda_Q = 1187\,\text{\AA} < \lambda_{\text{fi}}$. This photon redshifts into resonance with the Lyman-$\alpha$ transition, $\lambda_{\text{fi}} = 1216\,\text{\AA}$, at an intervening redshift
$$
z \simeq 1187 \times \frac{1+z_Q}{1216} - 1 = 1187 \times \frac{4}{1216} - 1 \simeq 2.9 .
$$
If neutral hydrogen is present at that location along the line of sight, it imprints a distinct absorption feature. This feature is observed today, redshifted all the way from the QSO, at
$$
\lambda_{\text{obs}} = \lambda_Q(1+z_Q) \simeq 1187 \times 4 \simeq 4742\,\text{\AA} .
$$
More generally, absorption arising at any intervening redshift $z$ appears in the observed spectrum at $\lambda_{\text{obs}} = \lambda_{\text{fi}}(1+z)$, so that a single QSO spectrum simultaneously probes the neutral hydrogen content of the IGM over a wide range of redshifts between the observer and the source.
\end{exampleblock}

\subsection{The Gunn-Peterson Effect} \label{subsec:gunn_peterson}

If the IGM retained even a modest neutral hydrogen fraction, essentially every wavelength blueward of the QSO's rest-frame Lyman-$\alpha$ line would be absorbed, producing a complete trough of zero flux rather than a forest of discrete lines; this is the \bfit{Gunn-Peterson effect}. Comparing the spectra of QSOs at progressively higher redshift, a non-zero transmitted flux is observed to persist down to $z \sim 5.5$, providing direct evidence that the bulk of hydrogen reionization was already complete by that epoch.

\smallskip

The cross-section for resonant Lyman-$\alpha$ scattering is so large that only a tiny neutral fraction is required to saturate the trough completely,
$$
F \propto e^{-\tau_{\text{GP}}} , \qquad \tau_{\text{GP}} \approx \left(\frac{\bar X_{\mathrm{HI}}}{10^{-5}}\right) ,
$$
with $\bar X_{\mathrm{HI}} = \bar\rho_{\mathrm{HI}}/\bar\rho_{\mathrm H}$ the mean neutral hydrogen fraction. Because a neutral fraction as small as $X_{\mathrm{HI}} \sim 10^{-4}$ is already sufficient to produce full absorption, the Lyman-$\alpha$ forest saturates very easily and cannot pin down the precise ionisation state of the IGM once it is mostly neutral; it can only place a lower limit, $X_{\mathrm{HI}} \gtrsim 10^{-4}$, on the neutral fraction at the redshifts where absorption is observed.

\subsection{Synthesis and Open Questions} \label{subsec:reionization_open}

The two main observational probes of reionization are, at first sight, in tension: QSO absorption spectra imply a substantially neutral IGM ($X_{\mathrm{HI}} > 10^{-4}$) as late as $z \sim 6$, while CMB constraints, under the simplistic assumption of instantaneous reionization, suggest a somewhat higher characteristic redshift, $z_{\text{re}} \sim 7$-$8$. The two results are, however, entirely consistent once the instantaneous approximation is relaxed: the data collectively favour an extended reionization process, beginning at $z > 8$ and completing around $z \sim 6$, rather than a single sharp transition.

\smallskip

Several fundamental questions nonetheless remain open. The precise epoch of reionization, that is, when sources first produced enough ionising photons to complete the process, remains loosely bounded to the broad range $6 \lesssim z \lesssim 20$. The nature of the transition, whether sudden or gradual, and whether homogeneous or highly inhomogeneous across different environments, is still actively debated. Finally, the dominant sources of reionization, most plausibly the first generation of stars and galaxies, but with possible contributions from accreting black holes or even more exotic particle physics, are not yet firmly established. Redshifted 21\,cm observations of neutral hydrogen (\cref{sec:ism_hi}), stretched into the $10$-$100\,\text{MHz}$ range and best pursued from radio-quiet environments such as the lunar far side, are expected to become the decisive probe of this epoch, complementing both the CMB and quasar absorption studies discussed above.
