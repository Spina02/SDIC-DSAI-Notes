\chapter{The Interstellar Medium} \label{ch:ism}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:ism_intro}

The \bfit{interstellar medium} (ISM) is the diffuse matter, gas and dust, that fills the space between stars within a galaxy. It is conventionally split into two components. The \textbf{circumstellar medium} is directly associated with individual stars, typically young, luminous objects or the end-of-life phases of stellar evolution, and includes reflection nebulae, HII regions, planetary nebulae, and supernova remnants. The \textbf{diffuse component}, by contrast, is not tied to any single star and instead pervades the general volume of the galaxy; its distribution can be traced, for instance, through 21\,cm HI emission mapped across the sky.

\subsection{Historical Recognition of the ISM} \label{subsec:ism_history}

The existence of an interstellar medium was inferred well before its physical nature was understood. In 1795, William Herschel, compiling the first catalogue of nebulae, noted conspicuous dark patches devoid of stars, famously describing one as "a hole in the sky", without realising that these were in fact opaque foreground dust clouds rather than genuine gaps in the stellar distribution. A more direct detection came in 1904, when Hartmann observed stationary \ion{Ca}{ii} absorption lines in the spectrum of the spectroscopic binary $\delta$~Ori: unlike the stellar lines, which shift periodically with the orbital motion of the binary, these absorption features remained fixed in radial velocity, indicating an origin in intervening gas rather than in the stellar atmospheres themselves. Plaskett and Pearce (1933) strengthened this interpretation by showing that the absorption strengthens with the distance of the background star, confirming an interstellar, rather than circumstellar, origin for the absorbing gas.

\smallskip

Independent evidence for a diffuse absorbing medium came from Trumpler's 1930 study of $\sim$100 Galactic open clusters. If clusters share, on average, a common physical size and luminosity, their distance can be estimated in two independent ways: from the angular diameter (assuming a fixed linear size) and from the apparent magnitude (assuming a fixed luminosity). Trumpler found that these two distance estimates diverge systematically for more distant clusters, with the angular-diameter distance falling below the photometric one. Since the Sun lies within the Galactic disk, both distance estimators should coincide in the absence of any additional attenuation; the observed discrepancy therefore signals the presence of a foreground extinguishing medium, quantified by

$$
m - M = 5\log_{10}(d/10\,\text{pc}) + A_V ,
$$

with $A_V \sim 1\,\text{mag}\,\text{kpc}^{-1}$ in the Galactic plane. A handful of further milestones completed the observational picture over the following decades: the first interstellar molecules (CH, CH$^+$, CN) were identified around 1937, van~de Hulst predicted the detectability of the HI 21\,cm line in 1945, interstellar polarisation revealed the presence of a Galactic magnetic field in 1949, and the first HI maps of the Milky Way followed in the 1950s, eventually revealing its spiral structure.

\section{Circumstellar Regions} \label{sec:circumstellar}

\subsection{Reflection Nebulae} \label{subsec:reflection_nebulae}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
\textbf{Reflection nebulae} arise when circumstellar gas and dust scatter the light of a nearby, relatively cool star. If the star is not hot enough to produce photons above the hydrogen ionisation threshold ($E > 13.6\,\text{eV}$), the surrounding gas remains neutral, and the dominant interaction is elastic scattering of starlight off dust grains ($d \sim 0.01$-$0.3\,\mu$m). Because scattering efficiency increases toward shorter wavelengths, the nebula appears distinctly blue. Their lifetime, of order $10^6$-$10^7\,\text{yr}$, is set by the duration of proximity to a luminous star (a B-type star lives $\sim 10^7\,\text{yr}$) and by the survival of the dusty envelope itself against stellar winds, radiation pressure, and supernova feedback.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering

    \captionof{figure}{The reflection nebula surrounding V838~Mon.}
    \label{fig:reflection_nebula}
\end{minipage}

\subsection{HII Regions and the Strömgren Sphere} \label{subsec:hii_regions}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
\bfit{HII regions} are low-density clouds of partially ionised gas surrounding short-lived, early-type stars. Ionisation proceeds through two channels: direct photoionisation by UV photons ($E > 13.6\,\text{eV}$) from the central O/B stars, and collisional ionisation in shocks driven by stellar winds. Because they trace recent star formation, HII regions are closely associated with giant molecular clouds and can extend up to $\sim 100\,\text{pc}$; their lifetime, $1$-$10\,\text{Myr}$, is set by that of the massive stars powering them ($M > 20\,M_\odot$). The primary optical diagnostic is the H$\alpha$ line, the $n=3 \to n=2$ Balmer transition at $656.28\,\text{nm}$.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \todo{Orion Nebula, HST visible+IR composite}
    \captionof{figure}{The Orion Nebula, a prototypical Galactic HII region.}
    \label{fig:orion}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

An HII region settles into ionisation equilibrium: the rate at which ionising photons are absorbed must balance the rate at which free electrons and protons recombine into neutral hydrogen. This balance defines the \bfit{Strömgren sphere}.

\begin{exampleblock}[The Strömgren radius]
Let $N$ denote the number of ionising photons emitted per second by the central star, $\alpha$ the recombination coefficient, and $n_H$ the hydrogen number density (assumed uniform). In equilibrium, the total recombination rate within a sphere of radius $r_S$ must equal the ionising photon rate:
$$
N = \frac{4}{3}\pi r_S^3\, n_H^2\, \alpha .
$$
Solving for $r_S$ gives the Strömgren radius,
$$
r_S \simeq \left(\frac{3N}{4\pi\alpha}\right)^{1/3} n_H^{-2/3} .
$$
Observed values of $r_S$ range from below $0.1\,\text{pc}$ in dense, compact regions to over $100\,\text{pc}$ in low-density environments, reflecting the strong sensitivity of $r_S$ to the ambient density.
\end{exampleblock}

\subsection{Planetary Nebulae} \label{subsec:planetary_nebulae}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
\textbf{Planetary nebulae} are expanding, photoionised shells of gas ejected during the late evolution of low- to intermediate-mass stars ($\approx 1$-$8\,M_\odot$). During the red giant phase, strong stellar winds expel the star's outer layers; once most of the envelope has dissipated, the exposed hot core emits UV radiation that ionises the ejected material, causing it to glow as a compact, brightly coloured nebula. Their lifetime is comparatively short, of order $10^4\,\text{yr}$, set by the expansion timescale of the shell.
\end{minipage}%

\subsection{Supernova Remnants} \label{subsec:snr}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
A \bfit{supernova remnant} (SNR) is the expanding shock structure left behind when a supernova explosion interacts with the surrounding interstellar or circumstellar medium. The explosion ejects much or all of the progenitor's mass at supersonic velocities up to $10^4\,\text{km/s}$; the resulting shock heats the swept-up plasma to $T > 10^6\,\text{K}$. As the remnant expands, it continuously sweeps up ambient material and decelerates, eventually merging into the general ISM once its expansion speed falls below the local sound speed, after tens of parsecs and a lifetime of $10^4$-$10^5\,\text{yr}$.
\end{minipage}%

\section{General Properties of the ISM} \label{sec:ism_generalities}

The ISM is best understood as a non-linear, self-regulated, multiphase system, in which gravity, turbulence, magnetic fields, and radiative processes jointly govern the baryon cycle of a galaxy. Although it hosts only a few percent of a galaxy's total mass (in the Milky Way, the ISM amounts to roughly $10$-$15\%$ of the stellar mass), its radiation, faint and emitted predominantly in the radio and infrared, encodes the physical conditions that regulate star formation. By mass the ISM is overwhelmingly gaseous ($\sim 99\%$ gas, $\sim 1\%$ dust), with a chemical composition close to primordial plus modest enrichment ($\sim 74\%$ H, $\sim 24\%$ He, $\sim 1$-$2\%$ metals by mass).

\medskip

The medium is continuously reprocessed: gas flows into the ISM through accretion, is converted into stars at a rate of order $1\,M_\odot\,\text{yr}^{-1}$ in the Milky Way, and is partially returned by stellar winds, planetary nebulae, and supernovae, enriched with the products of stellar nucleosynthesis and carrying substantial energy and momentum. This continuous cycling means that the ISM's density and temperature structure, in turn, feed back on the star formation rate of the galaxy.

\medskip

A defining characteristic of the ISM is that it exists in \bfit{multiple thermal phases} coexisting in rough pressure equilibrium, from cold molecular clouds at $T \sim 10$-$50\,\text{K}$ and densities of $10^3$-$10^6\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, through the cool and warm neutral media (CNM, WNM) at $T \sim 10^2$-$10^4\,\text{K}$, to diffuse HII gas at $T \sim 10^4\,\text{K}$ and the hot, collisionally ionised coronal gas at $T \gtrsim 3\times10^6\,\text{K}$. Despite their vastly different temperatures and densities, these phases are linked by comparable thermal pressures, $p/k_B \sim 10^3$-$10^4\,\text{K}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, a signature of the rough mechanical equilibrium that allows such disparate gas to coexist within the same galactic volume. The ISM is further characterised by a very large range of ionisation states, complex geometry and kinematics, a significant dust content, weak magnetic fields ($B \sim 5\,\mu\text{G}$), pervasive radiation fields, and a substantial population of cosmic rays; because mean free paths in this rarefied gas are very long, strict thermodynamic equilibrium is rarely achieved.

\section{Interstellar Dust} \label{sec:ism_dust}

\subsection{Properties and Lifecycle} \label{subsec:dust_properties}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
Dust grains have typical sizes of order $0.1\,\mu\text{m}$ and constitute only $\sim 1\%$ of the total ISM mass, yet they play a physical role far out of proportion to this small mass fraction. Grains are mainly composed of \textbf{silicates} (large grains, $\lesssim 1\,\mu\text{m}$), \textbf{graphitic carbon} (small grains, $\sim 100$\,\AA), and \bfit{polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons} (PAHs, molecules of fewer than 100 atoms with characteristic aromatic ring structures).
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \todo{SEM photos of presolar grains: SiC, graphite, spinel, silicate}
    \captionof{figure}{Presolar dust grains recovered from primitive meteorites.}
    \label{fig:presolar_grains}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Dust is not a static component of the ISM but is continuously processed through a \bfit{dust lifecycle}: grains condense in the outflows of AGB stars and Type~II supernovae, are subsequently reshaped within the ISM through sputtering, shattering, coagulation, and icing as they cycle between diffuse and molecular phases, and are eventually incorporated into new stars (\textbf{astration}), closing the loop.

\subsection{Physical Effects of Dust} \label{subsec:dust_effects}

Dust grains influence the ISM and its observable properties in several distinct ways. They cause \bfit{extinction and reddening} of starlight, absorbing and scattering ultraviolet and optical photons more efficiently than infrared ones; the absorbed energy is re-emitted thermally in the far-infrared, from grains typically heated to $10$-$20\,\text{K}$ in the diffuse ISM (corresponding to $\lambda \sim 100$-$200\,\mu\text{m}$). This thermal emission also makes dust an effective \textbf{coolant} for molecular clouds. Grains further act as a \textbf{catalyst}, providing the primary surfaces on which H$_2$ forms, a process critical to star formation, and they drive \textbf{metal depletion}, locking refractory elements such as Ca, Fe, Al, and Si out of the gas phase.

\smallskip

Because interstellar grains are typically elongated and become aligned with the Galactic magnetic field, they introduce a \bfit{polarisation-dependent} response: starlight vibrating parallel to the grain axis is preferentially absorbed or scattered, so that light transmitted through an aligned dust cloud emerges polarised along the field direction, while the thermal emission from the same grains is polarised perpendicular to it.

\medskip

Quantitatively, dust extinction is a wavelength-dependent attenuation of starlight, strongest in the ultraviolet and weakening toward the infrared, observable from about $0.1$ to $20\,\mu\text{m}$ and superposed with spectral features (most notably a bump near $2175$\,\AA, of still-debated origin, possibly related to graphite or PAHs) that encode grain composition. The attenuation is conventionally parametrised through the \bfit{colour excess},
$$
E(B-V) = (B-V)_{\text{obs}} - (B-V)_{\text{true}} ,
$$
and the extinction in the visual band,
$$
A_V = R_V\, E(B-V), \qquad R_V \simeq 3.1 ,
$$
with the Galactic average $R_V = 3.05 \pm 0.15$. The wavelength dependence of the extinction follows
$$
A_\lambda = 2.5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{F_0(\lambda)}{F(\lambda)}\right) = R(\lambda)\,E(B-V) .
$$

\subsection{Observing Dust: Foreground Extinction Maps} \label{subsec:dust_observations}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
Because extinction is dominated by dust within our own Galaxy (foreground extinction), mapping it accurately along every line of sight is essential for correcting extragalactic observations. The \textbf{DIRBE}/\textbf{FIRAS} instruments on \textit{COBE} (1989-1993) provided one of the first direct measurements of the diffuse infrared sky in the $125$-$5000\,\mu\text{m}$ range. The \textbf{IRAS Sky Survey Atlas} (ISSA) subsequently scanned the Galaxy at 12, 25, 60, and $100\,\mu\text{m}$, from which dust column density, and hence $E(B-V)$, could be inferred using the intrinsic colours of elliptical galaxies as calibrators, converted to extinction via the standard $R_V = 3.1$.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.43\textwidth}
    \centering
    \todo{DIRBE 1.25/2.2/3.5 micron composite of the Milky Way}
    \captionof{figure}{DIRBE composite image of the Milky Way at 1.25, 2.2, and $3.5\,\mu\text{m}$.}
    \label{fig:dirbe}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

More recently, \textbf{Planck} measured dust emission across nine channels from 30 to $857\,\text{GHz}$ ($10$-$0.35\,\text{mm}$), allowing an accurate separation of foreground and background emission and, since interstellar dust is not isothermal, the most detailed maps to date of "cold" versus "warm" dust regions, essential for computing $A_V$ along individual lines of sight. Planck's polarisation measurements additionally enabled the first global mapping of the Galactic magnetic field via its imprint on dust emission.

\medskip

Because dust is concentrated in the Galactic disk but present in every direction, extinction toward any extragalactic source must be obtained by integrating along the full line of sight; it is most intense toward the Galactic Bulge, where the density of both dust and stars peaks, and correspondingly weaker toward the Galactic poles.

\subsection{Dark Nebulae} \label{subsec:dark_nebulae}

The dark patches noted qualitatively by Herschel are now understood as opaque clouds of dust, ranging from tiny \bfit{Bok globules} ($\sim 0.01\,\text{pc}$) to structures tens of parsecs across. Some remain dark even in the mid-infrared, and are termed \textbf{Infrared Dark Clouds} (IRDCs); nonetheless, these same clouds are often bright in the far-infrared, where the thermal emission of dust heated to $10$-$20\,\text{K}$ peaks.

\section{Molecular Clouds} \label{sec:ism_molecular}

\begin{minipage}{0.56\textwidth}
\bfit{Molecular clouds} represent the coldest and densest phase of the ISM, with densities of $100$-$1000\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, temperatures around $10\,\text{K}$, and internal velocities of order $10\,\text{km/s}$ dominated by supersonic turbulence. Individual giant molecular clouds can reach masses of $M \sim 10^6\,M_\odot$ and radii of $R \sim 50\,\text{pc}$, with a characteristic surface density $\Sigma_{\text{gas}} \sim 100\,M_\odot\,\text{pc}^{-2}$. Their primary constituents are H$_2$, CO, heavy elements (C, O, N), and dust grains.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.4\textwidth}
    \centering
    \todo{Eagle Nebula (M16), Pillars of Creation, HST}
    \captionof{figure}{The Eagle Nebula (M16), showing dense molecular pillars.}
    \label{fig:eagle_nebula}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

Despite being the most abundant molecule in the Universe, H$_2$ is symmetric and possesses no permanent electric dipole moment; it radiates only when shocked or irradiated to $T \gtrsim 1000\,\text{K}$, well above the typical $10$-$100\,\text{K}$ of molecular clouds, making direct detection of the bulk of the molecular gas essentially impossible. Polar molecules, by contrast, possess a non-zero dipole moment and radiate through rotational and vibrational transitions. For a diatomic molecule, the rotational energy at quantum level $J$ is
$$
E_{\text{rot}} = \frac{J(J+1)\hbar^2}{2I}, \qquad J = 0,1,2,\dots ,
$$
with the selection rule $\Delta J = \pm 1$ and emitted frequency
$$
\nu = \frac{\hbar J}{2\pi m r_{\text{eq}}^2} .
$$

\medskip

Because H$_2$ itself is inaccessible, molecular gas is instead traced through \bfit{CO}, the second most abundant molecule in the Galaxy. The $J=0 \to 1$ transition falls at $\lambda = 2.6\,\text{mm}$ ($115\,\text{GHz}$) and the $J=1 \to 2$ transition at $\lambda = 1.3\,\text{mm}$ ($230\,\text{GHz}$), with excitation temperatures of $\sim 11\,\text{K}$ and $\sim 17\,\text{K}$ respectively; the critical density for the $J=1$-$2$ transition, $\sim 700\,\text{cm}^{-3}$, is typical of Galactic giant molecular clouds. The conversion between integrated CO intensity and H$_2$ column density is calibrated empirically as
$$
X_{\text{CO}} = 2\times10^{20}\;\text{molecules\,cm}^{-2}\,(\text{K\,km\,s}^{-1})^{-1} .
$$
This factor is roughly constant across the Milky Way, but is not expected to be universal in other galaxies, particularly at low metallicity, making it a significant source of systematic uncertainty in extragalactic molecular gas studies.

\begin{observationblock}[Molecular gas in the Milky Way (Heyer \& Dame 2015)]
CO surveys imply a total H$_2$ mass of $(1.0 \pm 0.3)\times 10^9\,M_\odot$ in the Milky Way, of which $60$-$70\%$ lies inside the solar circle ($R_\odot = 8.5\,\text{kpc}$). The molecular gas is confined to a thin Gaussian layer around the Galactic plane, with a FWHM thickness of only $\sim 90\,\text{pc}$ in the inner Galaxy, broadening to several hundred parsecs beyond $10\,\text{kpc}$; on larger scales, molecular gas is found preferentially within the Galactic spiral arms.
\end{observationblock}

\section{Atomic Hydrogen and the 21\,cm Line} \label{sec:ism_hi}

Hydrogen is the most abundant element both in the Universe and in the ISM of the Milky Way, yet the cold neutral gas emits no visible radiation. Instead, neutral atomic hydrogen (\bfit{HI}) radiates through a hyperfine transition between two closely spaced sublevels of its ground state, arising from the coupling between the electron's spin and the proton's nuclear spin: the higher-energy configuration, with the two spins aligned, spontaneously flips to the lower-energy, anti-aligned configuration, emitting a photon at radio wavelengths.

\medskip

\begin{minipage}{0.5\textwidth}
The rest frequency of this transition is fixed by quantum mechanics,
$$
\nu_{10} = \frac{8}{3}g_I\left(\frac{m_e}{m_p}\right)\alpha^2(R_M c) = 1420.406\,\text{MHz} ,
$$
corresponding to a wavelength of $21.106\,\text{cm}$. The associated Einstein $A$-coefficient is remarkably small, $A = 2.87\times10^{-15}\,\text{s}^{-1} = (11\,\text{Myr})^{-1}$, meaning that an individual hydrogen atom spends on average $\sim 10^7\,\text{yr}$ in the excited sublevel before spontaneously de-exciting. The line remains observable across the Galaxy only because the sheer quantity of interstellar hydrogen compensates for the intrinsic weakness of the transition per atom.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.44\textwidth}
    \centering
    \todo{Hyperfine splitting diagram of atomic hydrogen ground state}
    \captionof{figure}{Hyperfine splitting of the hydrogen ground state; the $S=1 \to S=0$ transition emits the 21\,cm line.}
    \label{fig:hyperfine}
\end{minipage}

\medskip

The 21\,cm line was predicted by van~de Hulst in 1947 and detected independently, and nearly simultaneously, in 1951: Ewen and Purcell built a horn antenna and first observed the line at Easter 1950, while Oort and his student Mueller, working with a repurposed wartime radar antenna, had their initial apparatus destroyed by fire and only rebuilt it in time to publish jointly with Ewen and Purcell the following year.

\subsection{Mapping the Milky Way in HI} \label{subsec:hi_mapping}

By measuring the Doppler shift of the 21\,cm line,
$$
z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{obs}} - \lambda_{\text{em}}}{\lambda_{\text{em}}} = \frac{\nu_{\text{em}} - \nu_{\text{obs}}}{\nu_{\text{obs}}}, \qquad v = cz ,
$$
one obtains the radial velocity of individual HI clouds, while integrating the observed brightness temperature over frequency yields the HI column density along the line of sight,
$$
N(\text{HI}) = 1.821\times10^{13}\int_{\text{line}} T\,\tau_v\,dv .
$$

\medskip

Reconstructing the spatial distribution of HI requires a kinematic model of Galactic rotation, $\omega(R)$: assuming interstellar clouds move on circular orbits at Galactocentric radius $R$ with angular velocity $\omega(R)$, the observed radial velocity at a given Galactic longitude can be converted into a cloud distance. This method is only valid for $R < R_\odot$, since a given line of sight samples a given radius twice for material inside the solar circle. Applying this technique to 21\,cm surveys provided the first proof of the Milky Way's spiral structure (Oort, Kerr \& Westerhout 1958), and continues to show that both neutral hydrogen and dust are largely confined to the spiral arms in external galaxies as well.

\medskip

Because HI in galaxy disks is generally optically thin, the 21\,cm line suffers little self-absorption, so the total HI mass follows directly from the integrated line flux,
$$
\mathcal{M}(\text{HI}) = 2.36\times10^5\,\mathcal{M}_\odot \times d^2 \int F_\nu\!\left[1421\,\text{MHz}\times\left(1-\frac{V_r}{c}\right)\right] dV_r .
$$
In practice, observations are made in narrow velocity channels, each spanning a few $\text{km/s}$, so that the full three-dimensional (position-velocity) structure of the gas can be reconstructed, as in the classic channel maps of external spiral galaxies.

\begin{observationblock}[21\,cm rotation curves and dark matter]
Applying this technique to external spiral galaxies, 21\,cm observations of rotation curves (Vera Rubin, 1970s) provided the second major piece of evidence, after Zwicky's dynamical mass estimate of the Virgo cluster, for the existence of \textbf{dark matter}: rather than declining in a Keplerian fashion ($v \propto R^{-1/2}$) at large radii, observed rotation curves remain approximately flat out to the edge of the HI disk, well beyond where the visible stellar and gaseous mass alone could account for the inferred gravitational potential.
\end{observationblock}

\medskip

Redshifted to cosmological distances, the 21\,cm line also opens a direct observational window on the early Universe: at $200\,\text{MHz}$ it probes $z \approx 6$, and at $80\,\text{MHz}$, $z \approx 17$, spanning the epoch of the first stars and galaxies (\textbf{cosmic dawn}). This is a principal science driver for next-generation radio facilities such as the SKA (\cref{subsec:facilities}), alongside galaxy evolution, large-scale structure cosmology, and tests of general relativity in the strong-field regime.

\section{Ionized Phases of the ISM} \label{sec:ism_ionized}

Beyond the neutral and molecular gas, a substantial fraction of the ISM is ionised, spanning a wide range of temperatures and densities. Two distinct ionised phases are recognised: a hot, collisionally ionised component associated with supernova activity, and a warm, photoionised component associated with massive star formation.

\subsection{Hot Ionized Medium} \label{subsec:him}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
The \bfit{Hot Ionized Medium} (HIM) is primarily created and energised by supernovae and the intense radiation of high-mass stars; it represents the hottest phase of the interstellar cycle, following the injection of processed stellar material ("stardust injection") into the surrounding gas. Its defining properties are a very low density ($\sim 10^{-3}\,\text{cm}^{-3}$), an extremely high temperature ($\sim 10^6\,\text{K}$, at which the gas is almost completely ionised), thermal velocities of order $100\,\text{km/s}$, and complex kinematics. It is traced observationally through X-ray emission and high-ionisation far-UV absorption lines such as \ion{O}{vi}, \ion{C}{iv}, and \ion{N}{v}.
\end{minipage}%
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.42\textwidth}
    \centering

\medskip

The HIM occupies a remarkably large fraction of the ISM by volume, roughly $50\%$, despite its low density, and has a vertical scale height of order $3\,\text{kpc}$, for which reason it is sometimes referred to as the hot "corona" of the galaxy. This gas is often buoyant, rising as bubbles of characteristic size $\sim 20\,\text{pc}$ and forming galactic fountains that extend well above and below the disk. It is collisionally ionised and cools, primarily via adiabatic expansion and X-ray emission, on timescales of order $1\,\text{Myr}$.

\subsection{Warm Ionized Medium} \label{subsec:wim}

\begin{minipage}{0.55\textwidth}
The \bfit{Warm Ionized Medium} (WIM), also referred to as the \textbf{diffuse ionised gas} (DIG), is ionised by photons that escape the boundaries of HII regions rather than by direct stellar radiation. It has a density of $\sim 0.2\,\text{cm}^{-3}$ and a temperature of $\sim 10^4\,\text{K}$, and represents a transition phase sitting between the hot, high-energy HIM and the cooler neutral and molecular clouds. Despite its modest density, the WIM constitutes roughly $90\%$ of all ionised hydrogen mass in the Galaxy ($\sim 10^9\,M_\odot$) and fills about $20\%$ of the ISM volume, since it extends well beyond the boundaries of individual star-forming regions.
\end{minipage}%

\medskip

The WIM produces a faint, pervasive H$\alpha$ emission, arising as electrons recombine with hydrogen nuclei in the warm gas, detectable in essentially every direction across the sky. In external galaxies, it is often resolved into discrete loops, filaments, and shells not directly coincident with any HII region, and accounts for $30$-$50\%$ of a galaxy's total H$\alpha$ emission. As with HI, this H$\alpha$ intensity is measured across narrow velocity channels, exploiting the Doppler effect to recover the kinematics of the ionised gas.

\section{Observing the ISM Across the Spectrum} \label{sec:ism_observing}

Because different physical processes and phases of the ISM dominate at different wavelengths, a complete picture of the medium requires decomposing the observed Galactic emission band by band. Moving from long to short wavelengths: \textbf{radio continuum} emission traces synchrotron radiation from relativistic electrons, accelerated by supernova shocks and pulsars, spiralling in the $\sim \mu\text{G}$ Galactic magnetic field, while at higher radio frequencies this synchrotron component is joined by \textbf{bremsstrahlung} (free-free) emission from supernova remnants and hot ionised gas. The \textbf{21\,cm line} isolates the hyperfine transition of neutral atomic hydrogen (\cref{sec:ism_hi}), while millimetre \textbf{CO} emission traces the molecular gas confined to the Galactic plane (\cref{sec:ism_molecular}).

\smallskip

Moving into the infrared, \textbf{far-infrared} emission is thermal radiation from dust, heated either diffusely by the general interstellar radiation field of old stars (producing the so-called Galactic "cirrus") or locally by young massive stars embedded in molecular clouds; \textbf{mid-infrared} emission is dominated by PAH features from the diffuse dust component (\cref{sec:ism_dust}); and \textbf{near-infrared} light traces the underlying stellar population (main-sequence and giant stars) largely unobscured by dust absorption. In the \textbf{optical}, stellar emission is instead significantly shaped by dust extinction along the line of sight. Finally, at the highest energies, \textbf{X-ray} emission arises from X-ray binaries and diffuse hot gas (partially absorbed by intervening neutral hydrogen), while \textbf{gamma-ray} emission combines compact sources with diffuse photons produced by cosmic-ray collisions with the ambient ISM gas.
